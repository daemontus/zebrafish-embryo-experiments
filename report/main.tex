\section{Glossary and preliminaries}

Because biology likes to use big, fancy words, we first have to build a glossary of terms that will appear in this report. Most are relatively ``standard'' in the literature, but many have slightly different meaning across subfields, so this section can be also viewed as term definitions.

\begin{itemize}
	\item A \textbf{transcription factor (TF)} is a protein that directly influences the transcription process, either by participating in it, or by modifying the environment that influences the actual transcription machinery (e.g., opening or closing chromatin). Note that this is intentionally broader than the strict textbook definition, which only covers proteins that bind specific DNA sequences; here, we also include co-factors and chromatin remodelers.
	\item Time points in zebrafish embryo development are usually denoted in \textbf{hours post fertilization (hpf)}. We will shorten this to \textbf{hours (h)} when clear from context. We will be mostly interested in the development processes taking place until approx. \textbf{12hpf}.
	\item Since zebrafish are simple organisms, the embryo development is also relatively stable and easy to reproduce. The most important environmental factor is \textbf{temperature}, which is typically \textbf{28.5 °C}. Other temperatures within acceptable rates should have roughly linear influence on the development speed \citationneeded. Other factors like pH, medium density or oxygen levels also impact development, but the embryo is robust to small, reasonable variations in them \citationneeded.
	\item An embryo cell can be \textbf{totipotent}, \textbf{pluripotent}, or \textbf{multipotent}. Totipotent means it can differentiate into internal embryo cells, or external cell layers enveloping the embryo (\textbf{extra-embryonic cells}). Pluripotent means it can differentiate into any embryo cell, but not into an extra-embryonic cell. Multipotent means the cell can differentiate within a specific lineage (e.g., endoderm), but not outside of that specific lineage (at least not without a significant intervention).
	\item We can also distinguish cells by how \emph{robustly} they are differentiated so far. Note that these terms are originally defined based on what happens to the cell across various environments, not necessarily by the epigenetics and transcriptome, so we are ``extrapolating'' a bit here to what is causing these terms to be well defined in the first place.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item A cell is \textbf{primed} if its epigenetics admit a particular differentiation event (relevant chromatin is open, important TF is expressed but not causing transcription yet, etc.). A cell can be primed towards many lineages at the same time. Importantly, a primed cell will not differentiate without some additional ``signal'' triggering the differentiation.
		\item A cell is \textbf{specified} if it is actively transcribing RNA of a particular cell type, and will continue this process in a neutral environment. In other words, the transcription has been started and is somewhat self-sustaining, but can be easily disrupted by an unexpected ``signal''.
		\item A cell is \textbf{determined} if it is specified, but it can maintain its own identity when encountering an opposing, but reasonable ``signal'' (i.e., a signal that would naturally occur somewhere in the organism). Determination can be driven by a stronger self-sustaining GRN feedback loop, as well as by epigenetic changes.
		\item A cell is \textbf{differentiated} if it is determined, and it has undergone morphological changes associated with its cell type, including additional epigenetic changes to further increase robustness against signaling perturbations.
		\item The expression profile of a specified / determined / differentiated cell can be different, despite being ``the same cell type''. The cell is actively producing TFs that will lead to epigenetic changes required for determination / differentiation, but this transcription can eventually turn off once the cell is ``sufficiently committed''.
		\item The process is hierarchical (cell type A differentiates into B, which turns into C, \dots), and for the intermediate cell types, the cell does not have to pass all stages. For example, pluripotent cells will never be truly differentiated, or even determined. Similarly, stages can interleave across cell types, so a cell can be determined type A, but specified type B, and primed type C, meaning it will maintain identity A on its own, is already developing expression profile of type B, and is able to eventually become type C if a signal is given.
	\end{itemize}

	\item A zebrafish embryo goes through several ``high-level'' developmental stages: \textbf{zygote (0-0.75h)}, \textbf{cleavage (0.75-2.25h)}, \textbf{blastula (2.25-5.25h)}, and \textbf{gastrula (5.25-10h)}.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item In the zygote and cleavage stage, the embryo looks mostly uniform. However, it is not fully symmetric: some maternal factors (dorsal determinants, germ plasm) are already localized asymmetrically (see MBT below).
		\item During blastula, the surface layer of extra-embryonic cells is starting to split off. These are the \textbf{enveloping layer} (EVL) and \textbf{yolk syncytial layer} (YSL); one guards the embryo from the environment, and one is an interface with the yolk on which the embryo rests. Remaining cells are also called \textbf{deep cells}.
		\item A \textbf{midblastula transition (MBT)} occurs around \textbf{2.75hpf}. Until that point, the cells are predominantly driven by the maternal transcriptome present in the zygote (a small first wave of zygotic transcription already starts around the 64-cell stage). Until MBT, the whole cell population is essentially synchronous. Nevertheless, several important decisions are already made in this period, driven by maternal factors:
		\begin{itemize}
			\item formation of the YSL and EVL (YSL forms at 512-1024 cells; Figure~\ref{fig:development});
			\item specification of a small group of \textbf{primordial germ cells}, which inherit maternal \textbf{germ plasm} during cleavage;
			\item the \textbf{dorsal-ventral} axis (see below), determined by maternal dorsal determinants that are transported to one side of the embryo.
		\end{itemize}
		\emph{The causes of these decisions mostly can't be observed through RNA expression} (only the consequences), since they rely on localization of maternal proteins. Also, \emph{the overall mass of the embryo does not change much} during blastula and gastrula (the cells mostly thin out and spread); actual growth comes later. After MBT, the cells divide asynchronously, hence there is no longer a clear cell doubling pattern.
		\item During gastrula, the embryo splits along the \emph{radial axis} (the deep--superficial direction, perpendicular to the embryo surface; not to be confused with the radial symmetry of the blastula around the animal-vegetal axis) into \textbf{hypoblast} (develops into \textbf{mesendoderm}) and \textbf{epiblast} (develops into \textbf{ectoderm}). Note that hypoblast and epiblast are purely positional terms: Both are part of the \textbf{germ ring}, with hypoblast being the inner layer (adjacent to the yolk), while epiblast is the outer layer (see also Figure~\ref{fig:development}). In other words, a cell can be a part of hypoblast and of mesendoderm; one is the location, the other is the expression profile. The germ ring is the \textbf{margin} of the embryo (you can see cells called ``marginal'', meaning they belong to this region). The process of separation into hypoblast/epiblast along the germ ring is called \textbf{internalization}. During gastrula, the epiblast still contains mesendoderm precursors that internalize later; only once internalization finishes does epiblast fully correspond to ectoderm. 
		\item Within the gastrula phase, the embryo also visibly splits along the \textbf{dorsal-ventral} axis (dorsal is back / spine, ventral is front / belly). This split is marked by a \textbf{shield} formation on the dorsal side of the embryo. Note that the dorsal side itself is determined much earlier (maternally, see MBT above); the shield is where it first becomes morphologically visible. This is again mostly a positional term (i.e., shield is not an expression profile). However, cells in the shield do produce various signaling molecules that reinforce the dorsal-ventral split, and later also the anterior-posterior separation (see below). Sometimes, they are called \textbf{organizer cells}.
		\item The term \textbf{lateral} (and its opposite, \textbf{medial}) is used in two related senses. During gastrula, it refers to position along the margin: viewed from the animal pole, the margin is a circle with the dorsal side (shield) on one end and the ventral side on the opposite end; the \emph{lateral} margin are the two sides in between (left and right). In the body, it refers to the distance from the midline (the future spine): \emph{medial} is close to the midline, \emph{lateral} is far from it. The two are connected, because during gastrula the cells converge towards the dorsal side, which becomes the midline of the body. A related term is \textbf{paraxial}, meaning directly alongside the midline (i.e., the body axis).
		\item After gastrula, the embryo undergoes \textbf{early segmentation}, where the split along the \textbf{anterior-posterior} (A-P) axis becomes morphologically visible (as with the other axes, A-P patterning, e.g., of the neural plate, already starts during gastrula). The A-P axis is often confused with the \textbf{animal-vegetal} axis, but these are different axes: animal-vegetal is the axis of the egg (animal pole = top, where the blastoderm sits; vegetal pole = bottom of the yolk), while A-P is the body axis, which only forms during gastrula. They are only roughly aligned (the animal region gives rise to anterior head structures, while the tail bud forms near the vegetal pole). In Figure~\ref{fig:development}, AP/VP mark the animal/vegetal pole, while Ant./Pos. mark anterior/posterior. Segmentation is beyond the scope of this document.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\item During blastula and gastrula, the embryo also undergoes \textbf{epiboly}, during which it envelops the connected yolk. This is again largely used as a timing / position marker, with Figure~\ref{fig:development} showing the individual stages. Note that the formation of a hypoblast and epiblast, as well as of a germ ring and shield are happening relatively quickly between 50\% and 60\% epiboly, but this is a morphological change; transcriptomic and epigenetic changes leading up to the formation are already happening sooner.
	
	\item Another note is that in terms of morphology and transcriptome, epiblast is essentially just the continuation of the existing deep cells, without major changes at the moment of separation. It is the hypoblast that represents a new lineage that is splitting off of epiblast in the marginal region. However, later during gastrula, the ectoderm itself splits into \textbf{neural} and \textbf{non-neural} ectoderm (the latter becoming the \textbf{epidermis}), driven by a signaling gradient along the dorsal-ventral axis, and this split is visible in the transcriptome.
\end{itemize}

\section{Transcriptomic and epigenetic timeline}

In the previous section, we described the important terms and formations which appear during embryo development. However, these are mostly \emph{morphological} terms dictating what the embryo looks like or what is happening externally. They do not capture how the cells are evolving internally, which typically precedes morphological changes quite a bit. Also, as we discussed, these do not map directly to any meaningful definition of ``cell types'' (i.e., a ``shield cell'' is not a well defined cell type). In this section, we thus summarize the \emph{expression programs} that we expect to observe in the transcriptome during the first 12 hours, roughly when they emerge or diminish, what they do, and which earlier program they develop from. A few general remarks:

\begin{itemize}
	\item All time ranges are approximate. They denote when a program should become \emph{distinguishable in the transcriptome}, which usually precedes the corresponding morphological change by an hour or more. Also, the transitions are gradual: a new program typically first appears as a weak, graded bias within its parent population, and only later becomes a clearly separated cluster.
	\item Programs rarely ``end''; most of them keep splitting into more specific sub-programs. When we say a program diminishes, we mean it is no longer a meaningful description of the cells, because they have been replaced by its more specific descendants.
	\item Not everything is equally visible in single-cell data. Some programs are not captured at all (e.g., the YSL, which is a syncytium, not a set of cells), or involve only a handful of cells (e.g., germ cells), and the GSE106587 atlas only starts at 3.3hpf, i.e., after MBT.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:programs} gives an overview, and Figure~\ref{fig:programs} shows the same programs as a timeline, highlighting the branching points and the spatial axis along which each split happens. The individual programs are described in more detail below.

\subsection{Cleavage and blastula (0-5.25hpf)}

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Maternal program} (0hpf, cleared by approx. 4-6hpf). Transcripts deposited in the egg by the mother. They drive the cleavage divisions and activate the zygotic genome. After MBT, a large fraction of maternal transcripts is actively degraded; by the start of gastrula, the transcriptome is dominated by zygotic expression \citationneeded. In the atlas, the earliest stages (high, oblong) should show a strong maternal component shared by all cells.
	\item \textbf{Blastomeres} (approx. 2-3hpf, diminishing 5-8hpf). The ``default'' program of deep cells after zygotic genome activation (ZGA). Cells are pluripotent and largely uniform; the observable differences between them are initially small and graded (distance from the margin, i.e., position along the animal-vegetal axis, and position along the dorsal-ventral axis). This program gradually disappears as the cells commit to germ layers; it persists longest in the epiblast, which is its direct continuation.
	\item \textbf{Yolk syncytial layer (YSL)} (2.75-3hpf, persists). Forms when marginal blastomeres collapse into the yolk cell. It drives epiboly and, importantly, is a source of signals that induce the marginal mesendoderm and contribute to the dorsal organizer \citationneeded. Because it is a syncytium inside the yolk, \emph{it is not captured in single-cell data}; we only see its effect on neighbouring cells.
	\item \textbf{Enveloping layer (EVL)} (approx.~3-4hpf, persists as \textbf{periderm}). Develops from the outermost blastomeres, which become lineage-restricted during the blastula \citationneeded. Forms a protective epithelium around the embryo. In single-cell data, it is expected to be the first clearly separate cluster, already at the earliest atlas stages.
	\item \textbf{Primordial germ cells (PGCs)} (specified during cleavage, own zygotic program from approx. 3-4hpf, persists). Blastomeres that inherited maternal germ plasm. They give rise to gametes and do not participate in somatic differentiation. Only a handful of cells per embryo, so they will form a very small cluster.
	\item \textbf{Dorsal organizer} (approx. 3.5-4.5hpf, splits at 6.5-8hpf). Dorsal marginal cells (together with the dorsal YSL) that respond to the maternally determined dorsal side of the embryo. They produce signals that dorsalize the surrounding tissue, protect the dorsal ectoderm from ventralizing signals (enabling neural fate), and later contribute to A-P patterning. Morphologically, this becomes the \textbf{shield} at 6hpf; transcriptionally, the program is present well before that. Its cells later give rise to the axial mesoderm (prechordal plate and notochord). From approx. 6hpf, the internalized organizer cells (the inner layer of the shield) are often called the \textbf{axial mesendoderm}, i.e., midline mesendoderm that has not yet split into prechordal plate and notochord. We do not treat it as a separate program, because it is transcriptionally a gradual continuation of the organizer program; the clear split only happens with the prechordal plate and notochord.
	\item \textbf{Marginal mesendoderm} (approx. 4-5hpf, splits at 6-7hpf). Cells within a few cell tiers of the margin, induced by signals from the YSL and neighbouring marginal cells. The program is graded: the closer to the margin (and the stronger the signal), the more the cells are biased towards endoderm, while cells further away are biased towards mesoderm \citationneeded. These are the cells that internalize to form the hypoblast.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Gastrula (5.25-10hpf)}

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Endoderm} (approx. 5.5-6.5hpf, persists). Develops from the marginal mesendoderm cells that received the strongest inducing signal (mostly close to the margin, more on the dorsal side). Will form the gut and associated organs (liver, pancreas) and the pharynx. Relatively few cells at this stage.
	\item \textbf{Dorsal forerunner cells} (approx. 5.5-6.5hpf, forming the \textbf{Kupffer's vesicle} at approx. 10-12hpf). A small group of cells at the dorsal margin, ahead of the shield, partially derived from the EVL \citationneeded. They do not internalize; instead, they migrate ahead of the margin and form the Kupffer's vesicle, which establishes the left-right asymmetry. Only a handful of cells.
	\item \textbf{Prechordal plate} (approx. 6-7hpf, persists). The anterior part of the axial mesoderm, derived from the leading (first internalized) cells of the dorsal organizer. It migrates towards the animal pole and contributes to head patterning. Its most anterior part becomes the \textbf{hatching gland} (approx. 10-12hpf).
	\item \textbf{Notochord} (approx. 6-7hpf, persists). The posterior part of the axial mesoderm, derived from the remaining dorsal organizer cells. Forms the midline rod that later supports the body axis, and is an important source of signals that pattern the neighbouring neural tube and paraxial mesoderm.
	\item \textbf{Paraxial mesoderm} (approx. 7-8hpf, starts to form somites at 10hpf). Mesoderm from the lateral margin that converges on both sides of the notochord. Splits into \textbf{adaxial cells} (directly adjacent to the notochord; slow muscle precursors) and \textbf{presomitic mesoderm}, which will be segmented into somites (muscle, skeleton).
	\item \textbf{Lateral plate mesoderm} (approx. 7-8hpf, splitting at 9-12hpf). Mesoderm from the ventral and lateral margin. Gives rise to the heart, blood and endothelial precursors, and the pronephros (kidney). Many of these sub-programs only become clearly distinct around the end of gastrula or early segmentation.
	\item \textbf{Non-neural ectoderm} (approx. 6-7hpf, persists as \textbf{epidermis}). The ventral part of the epiblast, where ventralizing signals are strong. It is the first ectodermal program to diverge from the blastomere program.
	\item \textbf{Neural ectoderm} (approx. 6-7hpf, subdivided at 8-10hpf). The dorsal part of the epiblast, protected from ventralizing signals by the dorsal organizer. Forms the \textbf{neural plate}. During late gastrula, it is patterned along the A-P axis into forebrain (including eye field), midbrain, hindbrain and spinal cord programs.
	\item \textbf{Neural plate border} (approx. 8-10hpf, splitting at 10-12hpf). Cells at the boundary between neural and non-neural ectoderm, receiving intermediate levels of signals. Gives rise to the \textbf{neural crest} (very diverse lineage: pigment cells, craniofacial skeleton, peripheral neurons, \dots) and \textbf{placodes} (sensory organs).
	\item \textbf{Tailbud} (approx. 9-10hpf, persists). Forms from ventral marginal cells converging at the vegetal pole at the end of epiboly. Contains bipotent \textbf{neuromesodermal progenitors} that produce both spinal cord and paraxial mesoderm during body axis elongation. Note that this is one of the few programs which mix ``neural'' and ``mesodermal'' signatures.
\end{itemize}

\section{Dataset overview}

The analysis is based on the GEO SuperSeries GSE106587~\cite{farrell2018}. It consists of three single-cell datasets:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Drop-seq atlas} (SubSeries GSE106474). Wild-type embryos at 12 stages from high (3.3hpf) to 6-somite (12hpf); 39{,}505 cells, 23{,}974 genes. This is the main dataset.
	\item \textbf{SMART-seq2} (SubSeries GSE106466). 416 cells at 50\% epiboly: 52 wild-type cells and 364 cells of MZ\emph{oep} mutants, which lack Nodal signalling and hence most of the mesendoderm. Fewer cells, but deeper coverage per cell.
	\item \textbf{10x Genomics} (GSE106587 itself). Wild-type (3{,}000 barcodes) and MZ\emph{oep} (2{,}200 barcodes) embryos at 6-somite (12hpf). The barcode lists are not QC-filtered, so not every barcode is a cell.
\end{itemize}
All three are available only as processed count matrices (raw reads were not downloaded). In the rest of this section, we only describe the Drop-seq atlas. \textbf{The GEO matrix has already passed the basic quality filters of the original study, but not its later outlier removal} (Section~\ref{sec:filtering}); here, we use all 39{,}505 cells without any additional filtering. The experimental details below are taken from the supplementary materials of the paper. The numbers, figures and table are produced by \texttt{scripts/01\_dataset\_structure.py}.

\subsection{Stages, batches and libraries}

The Drop-seq atlas has three nested levels (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset-structure} and Table~\ref{tab:libraries}):
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Stage}: the developmental time point (12 stages, each with a single hpf value).
	\item \textbf{Sequencing batch} (DS2, DS3, DS4 and DS5): the ``sequencing batch'' field of the GEO samples. It is the main expected source of technical variation.
	\item \textbf{Library}: one sample of pooled embryos, processed in one Drop-seq run; equivalently, one GEO sample (GSM) and one SRA experiment. There are 28 libraries in total. Most (stage, batch) pairs have exactly one library. Six pairs have two, in which case the cell names of the second library carry a \texttt{b} suffix (e.g., \texttt{ZF50\_WT\_DS4b\_...}).
\end{itemize}

Each library was prepared from a pool of 20--40 embryos of the same stage. The embryos were manually deyolked (\textbf{which also removes the YSL}), mechanically dissociated, and the resulting cell suspension was encapsulated in Drop-seq droplets. Consequently, cells carry no information about which embryo (or which position in the embryo) they came from. Some libraries were sequenced twice to increase depth, but the reads of such re-sequencing are merged into the same library; they do not form a second library.

\paragraph{Second libraries are separate embryo pools.} GEO itself does not say whether the second library of a (stage, batch) pair comes from a separate embryo pool (biological replicate) or is a second run of the same pool (technical replicate). However, the supplementary Table~S1 of the paper lists the total number of embryos used for each stage (Table~\ref{tab:filtering}). For high (56 embryos), oblong (76) and 30\% epiboly (59), this is more than a single pool of at most 40 embryos, so the two libraries of these stages must come from different pools. For the remaining stages with a second library (50\% epiboly, bud and 6-somite), the embryo counts are consistent with separate pools of approx.~20 embryos each, but they do not rule out a shared pool. The paper also treats each library as its own unit: Table~S1 calls the libraries ``batches'' (two for high, four for 50\% epiboly), and the two 6-somite libraries are described as ``acquired in two batches'' and batch-corrected before trajectory reconstruction. We thus treat second libraries as biological replicates sequenced in the same sequencing batch. Note that the paper's ``batch'' corresponds to our library, not to the GEO sequencing batch.

\paragraph{GEO replicate numbering.} The ``Rep~$N$'' in the GEO sample titles numbers the libraries of a stage \emph{across batches}. It does not correspond to our $a$/$b$ label. For example, ``50\% epiboly Rep~2'' is the DS3 library, while ``high Rep~2'' is the second DS5 library of the high stage.

\subsection{Batch design}

The most important property of the atlas is that the batches split the stages into two blocks that \emph{share no stage}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Block A} (batch DS5 only): high, oblong, dome, 30\% epiboly, shield, 3-somite and 6-somite; 11 libraries, 10{,}328 cells.
	\item \textbf{Block B} (batches DS2, DS3 and DS4): 50\% epiboly, 60\%, 75\% and 90\% epiboly, and bud; 17 libraries, 29{,}177 cells. Every stage of this block was sequenced in all three batches.
\end{itemize}
Within block B, the design is balanced, so stage and batch effects can be separated. Within block A, there is only one batch, so stage differences are not confounded with batch, but there is also nothing to estimate the DS5 batch effect from. This is particularly unfortunate for our focus on the decisions around 6hpf. Shield (6hpf, DS5) lies between 50\% epiboly (5.3hpf) and 60\% epiboly (7hpf), which are both in block B. A batch effect will therefore appear as an abrupt developmental change at shield and back again at 60\% epiboly. 

\subsection{Library size and sequencing depth}

The number of cells per stage ranges from 200 (oblong) to 7{,}162 (bud) (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset-structure}). The five block B stages (50\% epiboly to bud) contain 74\% of all cells. The two earliest stages are tiny: high and oblong have 511 cells together, and three of their four libraries have fewer than 150 cells (48, 72 and 128). Sequencing depth (Figure~\ref{fig:library-depth}) is a median of 3{,}798 UMIs and 1{,}444 detected genes per cell over the whole atlas. We can make the following observations:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Depth decreases with developmental time}, from library medians of 4--13 thousand UMIs per cell at high and oblong to 2--3 thousand at 3- and 6-somite. Part of this is biological (cells get smaller and contain less mRNA with each division, which is also why the original study used stage-specific filtering thresholds).
	\item \textbf{Libraries of the same stage and batch are not interchangeable.} At oblong, the median depth of the first library is 3.3$\times$ that of the second; at high, the second library is 2$\times$ deeper than the first, and at 6-somite 1.6$\times$.
	%\item \textbf{Batch DS3 trades depth for cells.} From 75\% epiboly to bud, the DS3 library is the largest of its stage (up to 4{,}759 cells at bud) and has the lowest median depth. This is a typical batch signature (more cells sequenced with a similar number of reads), and block B allows us to measure it.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cell filtering}
\label{sec:filtering}

The original study filtered the cells in two steps; the dataset includes the first, but not the second filtering.

\paragraph{Basic quality filters (already applied in GEO).} First, cells were filtered by the number of detected genes and UMIs, using a separate range for each stage (Table~\ref{tab:filtering}), because RNA content per cell decreases over early development. Cells below the range are likely low-quality (e.g., broken cells or empty droplets with ambient RNA), while cells above the range are likely doublets. Afterwards, cells with more than 45\% mitochondrial RNA (a sign of stressed or dying cells) were removed. The GEO matrix is consistent with these filters having been applied:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Only 51 of the 39{,}505 cells fall outside the gene ranges, all of them just slightly.
	\item The mean number of detected genes per stage agrees with Table~S1 to within 1.5\%.
	\item The GEO matrix contains no mitochondrial genes. This explains why the mean UMIs per cell are 20--25\% lower than in Table~S1 (where approx.~20--29\% of UMIs are mitochondrial), and why some cells fall below the UMI ranges.% It also means we cannot check the mitochondrial filter ourselves.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Outlier removal (not applied in GEO).} Before the trajectory reconstruction, the paper removed 618 cells forming a cluster dominated by an ``apoptotic-like'' expression program, which appears in cells of many different types and would therefore create artificial connections between unrelated cell types. It also removed 139 cells that were poorly connected in the nearest-neighbour graph. The paper's full-data visualization uses 39{,}488 cells, and $39{,}488 - 618 - 139 = 38{,}731$, which is the number of cells reported in the paper. The GEO matrix contains 17 cells more than 39{,}488, which the paper does not explain. The identities of the removed cells are not available in GEO. Finally, the per-stage cell counts in Table~S1 sum to 38{,}671, i.e., 60 fewer than 38{,}731, so the paper is not fully consistent here either.

\subsection{Consequences for the analysis}

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Quantitative analysis within library, qualitative comparison across libraries.} To avoid batch and stage effects, quantitative analysis (normalization, binarization, etc.) should be calibrated and applied within individual libraries. Between libraries, we should primarily draw conclusions from qualitative results (e.g., gene A is higher than B in cells X across DS2/DS3/DS4; the quantity can be different, but the effect stays the same). 
	\item \textbf{When to compare quantities across libraries?} We will most likely need to break the first rule in a few places. First, comparing \textbf{time stages within batch} is reasonable when assessing long-term changes in transcription (but should probably carry greater burden of proof than within-library tests). Similarly, \textbf{batch-effect calibration within stage} is possible for DS2, DS3 and DS4. This is not possible with respect to DS5, but maybe we can at least extrapolate some useful information from it. Batch correction ideally stays within each stage to avoid ``correcting for'' biological development. But most algorithms can take this into account, so in theory, for DS2/DS3/DS4, a full batch correction run is viable; however, it is questionable whether it is needed to derive useful qualitative comparisons. Finally, \textbf{library-effect within batch and stage} can be also quantified using the $a$/$b$ replicates, but we only have these for a small number of libraries, and they do come from separate embryo pools, so there can be genuine biological differences between them.
	\item \textbf{Some pseudo-libraries may be needed.} The early development stages (high, oblong) contain a very small number of cells, further subdivided into the $a$/$b$ replicates. Assuming we don't observe significant expression profile differences between the $a$/$b$ replicates, it may be reasonable to merge them and treat them as one library to obtain a bigger cell pool for the following experiments. In this context, \textbf{down-sampling or weighting the remaining, much larger libraries may be necessary} for some analysis methods to avoid over-representation of the 50\%/60\%/75\%/90\%/bud stages.
	\item \textbf{Outliers must be handled by us.} The GEO matrix still contains the apoptotic-like cells and the poorly connected outliers removed in the paper. We likely need our own (comparable) outlier filtering step. Furthermore, some cell types not relevant for the Boolean model may be also eliminated outright (e.g. the germ cells, or the EVL). However, for operations such as discretization, it may be useful to have the whole expression distribution, even if it is not fully assigned to cell types.
\end{itemize}

\section{Result: markers of heterogeneity within libraries}
\label{sec:heterogeneity}

Many of the following analyses need a set of ``interesting'' genes: genes that define a low-dimensional embedding of the cells, genes whose expression we try to predict, or genes that pass a filter because they mark a subpopulation. The standard choice is a fixed number of \emph{highly variable genes} (HVGs). In this section, we replace it with a test that asks, for every gene and library, whether the gene varies between the cells of the library more than a gene expressed equally in every cell would. Intuitively, we expect genes that define specific subpopulations of the library to fall within such a subset. \textbf{We call such genes \emph{heterogeneous}} (within a library).

The test relies on the assumption that the \textbf{UMIs of a uniformly expressed gene should follow the negative-binomial distribution}, which accounts for the variance of bursty gene expression (the telegraph model) and sampling noise~\cite{grun2014noise,raj2006bursting}. The test quantifies the distance of the real expression from such an ideal distribution. The cutoff is calibrated against simulated libraries of uniformly expressed genes, which leaves approx.~10 false positives per library, and combined with an effect size estimated from the library itself, which gives three tiers of genes with an estimated false discovery rate of 50\%, 10\% and 1\% (weak, moderate, strong). \textbf{The test selects 421--1{,}527 genes per stage} (weak tier, dome onward; high and oblong are mostly uniform with 33--164 het.~genes per library). We show that the test is more consistent across comparable libraries than HVGs and it aligns better with genes with a localized expression pattern according to ZFIN~\cite{bradford2022zfin} records; the advantage is large over the original Seurat HVGs and moderate over newer HVG methods. The numbers, figures and tables are produced by \nolinkurl{scripts/01b_heterogeneous_genes.py}, \nolinkurl{scripts/01c_heterogeneity_zfin.py} and \nolinkurl{scripts/01d_hvg_flavours.py}.

\subsection{A model of a homogeneous gene}

Consider one library and one gene. Cell $i$ has a relative depth $s_i$ (its number of UMIs divided by the library average); in this dataset, $s_i$ varies by approx.~10$\times$ within a library. The variation in $s_i$ is mostly technical (capture efficiency), but it also absorbs differences in cell size: we only assume that a homogeneous gene makes up the same fraction of every cell's transcripts. Therefore, if the gene were to be expressed at the same level in every cell, cell $i$ would contain on average $s_i \cdot m$ of its UMIs, where $m$ is the gene's mean per average cell. However, the observed count of such a homogeneous gene is still random for two reasons:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Sampling.} Only a fraction of the transcripts of a cell is captured and sequenced. Given the true expression, the captured count follows Poisson distribution, with variance $s_i \cdot m$.
	\item \textbf{Biological noise.} Even in identical cells, the true expression is not identical: transcription occurs in bursts~\cite{raj2006bursting}, and cells can differ in size and cell-cycle stage. If the true expression varies between cells with a squared coefficient of variation $\varphi$ (gamma-distributed), the count is negative binomial (NB), with variance $s_i \cdot m + \varphi \cdot (s_i \cdot m)^2$.
\end{itemize}
NB counts describe droplet-based UMI data well, without any need for extra ``dropout'' zeros~\cite{grun2014noise,svensson2020zeros,hafemeister2019sctransform}. Meanwhile, the Poisson model ($\varphi = 0$) is not a useful baseline: For a well-expressed gene, the extra variance $\varphi \cdot (s_i \cdot m)^2$ is small in each cell, but with a thousand cells it is easily detectable: at shield, 88\% of the genes with 100--200 UMIs have $\hat\varphi > 0$. Under the Poisson model, nearly every well-expressed gene would have higher variance than expected. In other words, if we only accounted for sampling noise (Poisson model), but not for the biological expression noise (negative binomial model), we would wrongly conclude most genes are heterogeneous.

However, in the NB model, we do not know the true biological value of $\varphi$ for each gene. Instead, we estimate its value based on \emph{typical genes with the same mean}, and then ask whether the observed $\varphi$ differs significantly from this ``typical'' $\varphi_t$ (in other words, we assume most genes are homogeneous, which allows us to estimate their $\varphi_t$ and use it to distinguish the heterogeneous ones). A gene restricted to a subpopulation has a much larger $\varphi$: if it is expressed in a fraction $f$ of the cells (and uniformly within them), $\varphi \approx 1/f - 1$, e.g., 9 for $f = 10\%$ (this is only the impact of the subpopulation; true $\varphi$ will also depend on the biological noise; consequently, $\varphi$ is not zero even if $f=100\%$).

\paragraph{Estimating the squared coefficient of variation.} We estimate $\varphi$ separately for every gene in every library. Let $y_i$ be the UMI count of the gene in cell $i$, where $i$ runs over all cells of the library (no cells are removed). Then we use the method of moments:
\[
	\hat\varphi = \frac{\sum_i (y_i - s_i \hat m)^2 - \sum_i s_i \hat m}{\hat m^2 \sum_i s_i^2},\qquad \hat m = \frac{\sum_i y_i}{\sum_i s_i}.
\]
Here $\hat m$ is the gene's mean per average cell, the first sum is the observed variance around the expected counts $s_i \hat m$, and the second sum is the part of it that Poisson sampling explains. Thus $\hat\varphi$ is the variance left after subtracting the sampling noise, divided by the scale at which $\varphi$ enters the variance. The estimate is $0$ when the gene varies exactly as much as sampling alone predicts, and negative when it varies less, which happens by chance.

We only estimate $\varphi$ for genes with at least 10 UMIs in the library (6{,}756--12{,}555 of the 23{,}974 genes, depending on the library). Below that, a gene is detected in a handful of cells and its variance says almost nothing about its distribution. Such a gene is considered \emph{not measured} in that library: it has no estimate and is never considered heterogeneous there. In particular, it also counts as not heterogeneous when we combine multiple libraries of a single stage (see below).

The \emph{typical} $\varphi_t$ at a given mean is the value of a dispersion--mean trend fitted to the genes of the same library (see next paragraph). The \emph{residual} of a gene is $\log_{10} \hat\varphi - \log_{10} \varphi_t$, i.e., $\log_{10}$ of the ratio of the two: 0 means typical, 1 means ten times more variable than typical. We use a ratio rather than a difference because $\varphi_t$ itself changes by orders of magnitude with the mean. An absolute difference of 0.5 would thus be negligible for rare genes but extreme for well-expressed ones. Meanwhile, ratio remains comparable across genes with vastly different $\varphi_t$. The ratio requires a positive $\hat\varphi$, so negative and zero estimates are floored at $10^{-3}$; such genes are far below any reasonable cutoff and are clearly not heterogeneous anyway. 

\paragraph{Typical overdispersion: a dispersion--mean trend.} In every library, genes of similar mean have similar $\hat\varphi$, and $\hat\varphi$ falls with the mean. We describe this by a trend with three parameters,
\[
	\varphi_t(m) = a_0 + a_1\, m^{-b}.
\]
Its shape follows from the mechanism of the noise. Under the telegraph model of bursty transcription~\cite{raj2006bursting}, a gene's NB shape parameter is its burst frequency and its mean is the burst frequency times the burst size, so $\varphi \approx$ burst size$/m$: rarer genes are noisier, approx.~as $1/m$ if burst sizes are similar. The constant $a_0$ is the noise floor shared by all genes (e.g., cell-cycle stage and cell size beyond what $s_i$ absorbs), which becomes comparable to the power law only for the most expressed genes; the same ``$a/\text{mean} + \text{floor}$'' pattern was found across the proteome of single \emph{E.~coli} cells~\cite{taniguchi2010quantifying}. Comparing every gene with this trend thus absorbs the variation that all genes share, without having to model each source.

The trend is fitted only to genes with at least 100 UMIs in the library. Below that, the estimate is dominated by chance: a gene with 10--30 UMIs in a library of 1{,}000 cells is detected in a few dozen cells, typically with one UMI each. For example, at shield, only 33\% of the genes with 10--15 UMIs have a positive estimate, compared with 88\% of the genes with 100--200 UMIs. For genes with fewer than 100 UMIs, $\varphi_t(m)$ is therefore a pure extrapolation. To check that the extrapolation does not depend on this choice, we refitted the trend to genes with at least 50 and 30 UMIs instead of 100: the typical value of the rarer genes changes by a median of at most 0.01 in $\log_{10}$ (at most 0.11, in the smallest library from dome on). At 20 UMIs, the fit breaks in some libraries, because the bins of such genes have medians at or below 0, i.e., the threshold must stay well above the point where most estimates are dominated by sampling.

The genes with at least 100 UMIs are sorted by mean and divided into bins of 200 genes, and $\log_{10}\varphi_t$ is fitted by least squares to the medians of $\hat\varphi$ in the bins (6--35 bins per library). The medians make the fit insensitive to heterogeneous genes, which are a minority of every bin. In the 24 libraries from dome on, the trend describes the bin medians within 0.03 in $\log_{10}$ (root mean square), with very similar exponents $b = 0.72$--$0.85$ (median 0.77), i.e., slightly less steep than $1/m$, as expected if burst sizes grow somewhat with expression. The floor is small ($a_0 = 0$--$0.026$, median 0.008): over the observed range, the trend is mostly the power law, and it levels off only for the most expressed genes. There, it is slightly too high in the late libraries (median residual of the 100 most expressed, non-heterogeneous genes $-0.15$ to $-0.22$ at 3- and 6-somite, $-0.11$ to $+0.07$ elsewhere), which makes the test more conservative for these genes. In the four libraries of high and oblong (48--263 cells), only 6--20 bins are available and their medians are close to the noise of the estimate; the fitted curves are flat or erratic ($b = 0$--$0.66$), the typical value of rare genes is unreliable (Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneity-libraries}), and which of their genes are selected depends mostly on the chance level and on high effect-size floors (see below).

\paragraph{Relation to DESeq2 and other methods.} The model is the one used for bulk RNA-seq by DESeq2~\cite{love2014deseq2}: NB counts with variance $\mu + \alpha\mu^2$, size factors that play the role of $s_i$, and a dispersion $\alpha$ (our $\varphi$) that is assumed to depend smoothly on the mean, with the parametric trend $\alpha(\mu) = a_0 + a_1/\mu$ (ours has a free exponent). The two methods use the trend in opposite ways. DESeq2 has few samples per condition, so each gene's dispersion is very uncertain; the trend serves as a prior towards which the gene-wise estimates are shrunk, so that the \emph{means} of the conditions can be compared reliably. Deviations from the trend are treated as noise, except for ``dispersion outliers'' (genes far above the trend), which are not shrunk because they plausibly have extra variability. We have hundreds to thousands of cells per library, so the gene-wise estimates are precise for well-expressed genes, and the deviations from the trend are exactly what we look for: our heterogeneous genes are the dispersion outliers of a single library. In single-cell data, sctransform~\cite{hafemeister2019sctransform} also regularizes NB parameters as a smooth function of the mean, but uses them mainly to normalize cells; the variance of its residuals is used to rank genes, without a calibrated cutoff; and Brennecke et al.~\cite{brennecke2013noise} select genes whose CV$^2$ lies above a trend of the same form, $a_0 + a_1/\mu$, in which, unlike in our $\varphi$, the sampling noise is included.

\paragraph{Selecting heterogeneous genes.} A residual above 0 does not yet make a gene heterogeneous. A homogeneous gene can lie above the trend for two reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Its estimate is noisy (\emph{sampling noise}), which matters most for rare genes; this is handled by a \textbf{chance level} obtained from simulated libraries.
	\item It is intrinsically noisier than the median gene of its expression level (\emph{intrinsic noise}, e.g., larger bursts), which matters most for well-expressed genes, where sampling noise is small; this is handled by an \textbf{effect-size floor} obtained from the spread of the real genes.
\end{enumerate}
A gene is heterogeneous in a library if its residual reaches the larger of the two.

\paragraph{Sampling noise: chance level.} Even relative to a sensible typical value, the residual of a rare gene is noisy. A fixed cutoff therefore does not work: a residual of at least 0.5 ($3.2\times$ typical) selects 953--1{,}977 genes in the libraries of more than 1{,}000 cells, but also 126--615 genes in a simulated library with the same depths $s_i$ and gene means $\hat m$, in which every gene is drawn from an NB with the typical $\varphi$ of its mean, i.e., in which no gene is heterogeneous; in the small high and oblong libraries, the simulation even reaches 54--78\% of the genes selected in the data (Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneity-checks}a). We therefore use such simulations to set the cutoff. Eight simulated copies of each library give, for every expression level, the 99.9\% quantile of the residual of a homogeneous gene (running quantile over the approx.~1{,}000 genes of most similar mean, i.e., 8{,}000 simulated values; the residual is taken against the typical $\varphi$ the gene was simulated with, so that the trend of $\varphi$ with the mean does not enter the quantile). This chance level rises smoothly towards rare genes: at shield, from $2.4\times$ typical above 1{,}000 UMIs to $13\times$ at 10--15 UMIs. The simulations are random; each library has its own fixed seed (derived from its name), so the results are reproducible, and with other seeds, 2--4\% of the genes selected in a library change (up to 15\% in the smallest libraries; tested on four libraries).

\paragraph{Intrinsic noise: effect-size floor.} The simulation gives every homogeneous gene exactly the typical $\varphi$, but real genes differ in their intrinsic noise even when they are expressed uniformly. Among well-expressed genes (at least 1{,}000 UMIs), the residuals of the bulk of the genes have a robust standard deviation of 0.26--0.38 (from dome on), approx.~3--4$\times$ more than in the simulation, while the chance level there is only $1.4$--$2.7\times$ typical. The chance level alone would thus select every gene that is somewhat burstier than the median gene. We therefore require a minimum effect size, derived from an \emph{empirical null}~\cite{efron2004large}: the residuals of the well-expressed genes (or of the 300 most expressed genes, if fewer are well expressed) are modelled as normal, with their median and robust standard deviation. For any floor, the estimated false discovery rate (FDR) is the number of well-expressed genes this null distribution would place above the cutoff, divided by the number of well-expressed genes that actually are above it. Each library gets three floors, the smallest ones with an estimated FDR of at most 50\% (\textbf{weak}), 10\% (\textbf{moderate}) and 1\% (\textbf{strong}); a gene belongs to a tier if its residual reaches the larger of the tier's floor and the chance level. In the four libraries of high and oblong, there are hardly any well-expressed genes, and the 300 most expressed ones still carry much sampling noise (standard deviation 0.35--0.64), so their floors are high (0.83--1.49 for weak), and at oblong, the three tiers coincide.

\paragraph{What the tiers mean.} Table~\ref{tab:heterogeneity-tiers} summarizes the tiers and the two kinds of false positives. The sampling-noise false positives are counted in an independent, ninth simulation; the simulation cannot produce intrinsically noisy genes, because all its genes have the typical $\varphi$. The FDR, in turn, refers only to well-expressed genes, where the empirical null can be measured: only 17\% (median) of the weakly heterogeneous genes are well expressed, so the intrinsically noisy ones are at most approx.~9\% of the weak tier. Since the bulk of well-expressed genes also contains genes with mild, real differences between cells (e.g., gradients), its spread, and hence the FDR, is if anything overestimated. For rarer genes, intrinsic noise cannot be measured under the sampling noise; there, the chance level is already 4--13$\times$ typical, at or above two standard deviations of the intrinsic spread among well-expressed genes (approx.~$4\times$), so it also excludes most of them.

\paragraph{Defaults.} \textbf{Heterogeneous} without qualification means the weak tier. We use it by default, i.e., we accept that up to half of the well-expressed heterogeneous genes may be merely noisier than typical, in exchange for missing few real markers; the moderate and strong tiers are available where a smaller, more reliable set is needed. Rare genes are selected if their variation is large: at shield, \emph{sox17} (endoderm) has only 26 UMIs, but a residual of 1.20 ($16\times$ typical), and is moderately heterogeneous. A gene is in a tier \textbf{at a stage} if it is in that tier in at least half of the stage's libraries (Table~\ref{tab:heterogeneity}). The overdispersion, typical value and cutoffs of every library are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneity-libraries}.

\paragraph{Zeros.} The same model also predicts how many cells should contain no UMI of a gene. For a cell with expected count $\mu_i = s_i m$, this probability is $(1 + \varphi \mu_i)^{-1/\varphi}$ under the NB, and $e^{-\mu_i}$ under Poisson. A gene expressed in only some cells has \emph{more} zeros than a typical gene of the same mean, because its UMIs are concentrated in fewer cells. Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneity-model}b shows these excess zeros at shield. Typical genes lie on the NB curve (0), and slightly above the Poisson curve, which predicts too few zeros. The heterogeneous genes have up to 40\% more cells without a UMI than expected (the figure is cut at 10\%), i.e., they are absent from a substantial part of the library. The excess grows with the mean, as an expressed gene becomes increasingly unlikely to be missed by chance; at the lowest means, zeros are expected anyway, so the zero fraction carries little information, and the variance (panel a) is the more sensitive measure. Excess zeros are a consequence of heterogeneity, not a criterion: a gene can be heterogeneous with few extra zeros, if its variance comes from graded expression levels or from a few cells with many UMIs (36 of the 421 heterogeneous genes at shield have fewer extra zeros than an NB gene exactly at the weak cutoff would).

\subsection{Why not highly variable genes?}

The original Seurat HVG method~\cite{satija2015seurat} (Seurat v1/v2, still the default of scanpy~\cite{wolf2018scanpy}) normalizes every cell to 10{,}000 UMIs, takes $\log(1+x)$, and ranks the genes by their dispersion (variance over mean) relative to genes in the same bin of mean expression, keeping the top 2{,}000. This has three problems:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Rare genes look variable.} After depth normalization and the logarithm, a gene with a single UMI in a few cells has a large variance relative to its tiny mean, and its bin contains only similarly rare genes. As a consequence, many HVGs are genes we cannot measure at all: per stage, 1{,}083--1{,}924 of the 2{,}000 HVGs are not heterogeneous, with a median of 5--22 UMIs in the \emph{whole stage}, and 761--1{,}074 of them (38--54\% of all HVGs) have fewer than 10 UMIs in every library (Table~\ref{tab:heterogeneity}). Accordingly, the HVGs that are not heterogeneous have no more zeros than a typical gene: at shield, only 13 of these 1{,}661 genes have more than 2\% extra zeros (median 0\%; Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneity-model}c), against a median of 2.2\% for the heterogeneous genes. Most of them are barely detected (median 0.005 UMIs per cell).
	\item \textbf{Depth is only removed from the mean.} Normalizing to a common total changes the scale, but not the sampling noise, which is larger in shallow cells; the log transform distorts it further~\cite{townes2019glmpca}. The NB model accounts for depth explicitly.
	\item \textbf{Pooled libraries.} Selected on the pooled cells of a stage, a gene that differs between libraries (e.g., the heat shock genes of 50\% epiboly DS4$a$, Section~\ref{sec:replicates}) is ``variable'' even if it is uniform within each library. Our test runs within each library.
\end{itemize}
Conversely, 82--610 heterogeneous genes per stage (from dome on) are not among the HVGs, although they are well expressed (median 120--1{,}578 UMIs in the stage).
Newer HVG methods fix the first two problems in part; we compare with them in Section~\ref{sec:hvg-flavours}.

\paragraph{Replication.} If a gene ranking reflects the cell types of a stage, it should be similar in two libraries of the same stage. Over all pairs of libraries of the same stage, the 1{,}000 genes with the highest residual overlap approx.~twice as much (Jaccard index 0.27--0.50 from 30\% epiboly on) as the 1{,}000 genes with the highest Seurat dispersion (0.14--0.29; Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneity-checks}c). The same holds for the sets we actually use: the weakly heterogeneous genes of two libraries overlap 0.38--0.61, against 0.16--0.28 for as many top Seurat genes. At high and oblong, neither replicates (0.06--0.21).

\subsection{Heterogeneous genes reflect known expression patterns}
\label{sec:heterogeneity-zfin}

A gene selection that replicates better across libraries than HVGs could still be the wrong one, e.g., it could favour genes with some technical property. As an independent check, we ask whether genes known to be expressed in a restricted part of the embryo at a given stage are heterogeneous at that stage, and whether genes known to be expressed everywhere are not. The knowledge comes from the expression records of the Zebrafish Information Network (ZFIN)~\cite{bradford2022zfin}, compiled from the literature and from large in situ screens. The numbers and table are produced by \nolinkurl{scripts/01c_heterogeneity_zfin.py}.

\paragraph{ZFIN records.} We use the wild-type expression records of ZFIN (downloaded on 2026-10-26; \nolinkurl{scripts/download_zfin.sh}). Each record states that a gene was detected in an anatomical structure (e.g., ``shield'', ``neural plate'', ``whole organism'') during a range of developmental stages, by a given assay, in a given publication. We keep:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{mRNA in situ hybridization} only. This is the assay that shows \emph{where} in the embryo a gene is expressed; other assays (e.g., RT-PCR) record the whole embryo by necessity.
	\item \textbf{Publications before 2018}, so that the records are independent of the atlas~\cite{farrell2018}. The year is taken from ZFIN's publication list or, for direct data submissions such as the in situ screens of Thisse et al., from the date in the publication identifier.
	\item \textbf{Genes of the atlas}: ZFIN genes are matched to the gene symbols of the count matrix via their current symbol or an unambiguous former symbol (the matrix often uses older names).
\end{itemize}
This leaves 76{,}143 records of 8{,}350 genes (of 84{,}705 in situ records of 10{,}247 genes from 4{,}263 publications).

\paragraph{Stages.} ZFIN stages are time intervals (e.g., ``Shield'' is 6--8hpf), and a record covers the time from the start of its first stage to the start of the stage after its last one. A record is assigned to an atlas stage if it covers the time of the atlas stage (the start of the ZFIN stage of the same name; 60\% epiboly, which ZFIN does not distinguish, at 7hpf, i.e., within ``Shield''). At each atlas stage, a gene is
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{restricted} if at least one of its records at that stage names a localised structure, i.e., any anatomy term except 15 broad terms (``whole organism'', ``unspecified'', ``ectoderm'', ``head'', ``trunk'', \ldots; \nolinkurl{data/curated/zfin_anatomy_programs.tsv}) and the yolk and YSL, which were removed with the yolk before dissociation;
	\item \textbf{whole organism} if all of its records at that stage name broad terms only, i.e., it was seen everywhere (or no more specific location was recorded).
\end{itemize}
We expect restricted genes to be heterogeneous, and whole-organism genes not. Neither expectation is strict: a restricted gene may be too rare to reach our cutoff, or expressed in a large domain (e.g., the whole neural plate), and a record may cover several stages of which only some show the restricted pattern. We compare three selections of the genes measurable at the stage (at least 10 UMIs in one of its libraries): the heterogeneous genes, the same number of top Seurat HVGs (HVG$_k$), and the usual 2{,}000 HVGs.

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:heterogeneity-zfin} lists all stages. High and oblong are not informative: ZFIN records the blastula almost only as ``whole organism'', so there are only 15 and 10 measurable restricted genes, and these stages have few heterogeneous genes (Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity}); we exclude them from the summary. From dome to 6-somite:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Restricted genes are heterogeneous 3--12$\times$ more often than an average gene} (32--61\% of them, median 46\%, while the heterogeneous genes are 4--13\% of the measured genes). In the moderate and strong tiers, 22--54\% (median 35\%) and 11--39\% (median 26\%) of them are selected.
	\item \textbf{The HVGs recover fewer restricted genes:} 16--40\% (median 27\%) for the same number of HVGs, and 28--57\% (median 41\%) for 2{,}000 HVGs, i.e., with 1.3--4.8$\times$ as many genes.
	\item \textbf{Whole-organism genes are depleted:} 1.4--4.4\% of them are heterogeneous (weak), 0.3--0.5$\times$ the rate of an average gene, and 0.8--2.4\% and 0.4--1.3\% in the moderate and strong tiers. The method therefore does not simply select expressed or well-studied genes.
\end{itemize}
The recall is highest during gastrulation (30\% epiboly: 61\%; 90\% epiboly: 57\%) and lowest at 3- and 6-somite (32--40\%), where ZFIN records many genes with organ-specific patterns that begin during segmentation and are recorded over long stage ranges (i.e., partially out of scope for the studied atlas).

\subsection{Comparison with newer HVG methods}
\label{sec:hvg-flavours}

The test is a variant of an established idea: compare the variance of a gene with that of genes of similar mean under a count model, as do the technical-noise model of Brennecke et al.~\cite{brennecke2013noise}, the current Seurat default (``vst'': the variance of the counts standardized by a fitted mean--variance trend~\cite{stuart2019seurat}) and the analytic Pearson residuals~\cite{lause2021pearson} (the variance of NB Pearson residuals with one fixed dispersion). The comparisons of the previous sections use the oldest method, so we repeat them for the two newer ones, each on the pooled cells of a stage, and also with the genes ranked within each library and then combined (the recommended use with several batches). As usual, these methods first drop genes detected in fewer than 3 cells; we also show the old method with this filter. Figure~\ref{fig:hvg-flavours} summarizes the stages from dome on (ranges in Table~\ref{tab:hvg-flavours}) (\nolinkurl{scripts/01d_hvg_flavours.py}):
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Most of the gap is the old method.} Of the ZFIN genes with a restricted pattern at a stage (Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity-zfin}), the top $k$ genes of the old method contain 16--40\%, those of both newer methods 25--56\% and our ranking by the NB residual 29--59\% ($k$ = the number of heterogeneous genes of the stage). In replication, the top 1{,}000 genes of two libraries overlap 0.14--0.29 (old), 0.29--0.49 (vst), 0.24--0.46 (Pearson residuals) and 0.29--0.50 (NB residual; median pair per stage). The newer methods are close to our ranking: ours is ahead at most stages by at most 0.05, and behind at a few (e.g., Pearson residuals replicate better at bud). The filter of rare genes explains only a small part of the difference.
	\item \textbf{Rare genes remain.} Even the newer methods put 380--790 genes with fewer than 10 UMIs in every library of the stage among their top 2{,}000 when selected on the pooled stage (ranked per library, fewer at some stages, e.g., 1 at bud for Pearson residuals); in the top $k$, still 90--330 (pooled) or 0--256 (per library). Our test does not rank such genes at all.
	\item \textbf{The calibrated set is better than any ranking of the same size.} The heterogeneous genes contain 32--61\% of the restricted genes, 2--9 percentage points more than the best ranking at every stage, also at single-library stages (shield 35\% vs.\ 27--32\%, 3-somite 32\% vs.\ 25--29\%), where it cannot profit from replication. The cutoff that depends on the mean (the chance level of Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity}) admits genes that a single ranking puts too low. And the calibration gives the number of genes (421--1{,}527 per stage), where an HVG method needs a fixed number such as 2{,}000.
\end{itemize}
We therefore keep the heterogeneous genes, but the advantage over modern HVG methods is moderate; most of what Table~\ref{tab:heterogeneity} shows is a weakness of the old method.

\subsection{Consequences for the analysis}

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{The heterogeneous genes of a stage replace the HVGs} wherever we need ``interesting'' genes: for embeddings and neighbourhoods, as evaluation targets, and as gene filters. By default, these are the weakly heterogeneous genes; where a smaller, more reliable set is needed (e.g., as markers of a population), the moderate or strong tier should be used. They are listed per stage and library, with their tier, in \nolinkurl{results/tables/heterogeneous_genes.tsv.gz} and \nolinkurl{..._calls.tsv.gz} (0: none, 1: weak, 2: moderate, 3: strong). The function \texttt{heterogeneity\_calls} in the script applies the same calibration to any subset of cells (e.g., half of a library).
	\item \textbf{The two earliest stages have little detectable structure.} High and oblong have 182 and 102 heterogeneous genes, with 3--8 false positives per library: with 48--263 cells per library, only strong differences can be told from chance, and the rankings of their two libraries hardly overlap. This agrees with the expectation that cells before dome are largely equivalent (apart from small populations such as the germ plasm-carrying cells: \emph{nanos3} is strongly heterogeneous at every stage where it is measured), but it also means that we have little power there.
	\item \textbf{The number of heterogeneous genes grows over development,} from 421--1{,}031 between dome and bud to 1{,}527 at 6-somite, as cell types diversify. Shield (421) and dome (429) are single libraries; at stages with several libraries, a gene must be heterogeneous in at least half of them.
\end{itemize}

\section{Result: \texorpdfstring{$a$/$b$}{a/b} libraries have distinct expression profiles}
\label{sec:replicates}

Six (stage, batch) pairs have two libraries, $a$ and $b$ (Table~\ref{tab:libraries}). If the two libraries of a pair were interchangeable, we could merge them, which would remove one level of the library hierarchy and, in particular, give larger cell pools for the tiny high and oblong stages. In this section, we test whether the two libraries of a pair contain the same population of expression profiles. \textbf{They do not: in every pair, the libraries can be told apart, in some pairs as clearly as two neighbouring stages, and neither depth normalization nor depth matching changes this.} We therefore keep the $a$/$b$ libraries separate. The numbers and figures are produced by \nolinkurl{scripts/02_replicate_mixing.py} (mixing) and \nolinkurl{scripts/03_replicate_differences.py} (gene-level evidence).

\subsection{Experiment}

For each pair, we embed the cells of both libraries together using a standard pipeline: each cell is normalized to 10{,}000 UMIs and log-transformed, the genes are restricted to those heterogeneous in either of the two libraries (Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity}; 74--1{,}527 genes per comparison), and the cells are projected onto the first 30 principal components using PCA. We do not use the usual highly variable genes: selected on two libraries jointly, they include genes that differ between the libraries as a whole rather than between cells, which would bias the test towards separation. In this space, we find the 20 nearest neighbours of every cell. If the libraries contain the same population of cells, the neighbourhood of a cell is a random sample of both libraries, i.e., the number of neighbours from library $b$ follows a binomial distribution around the overall share of $b$ cells. If one library contains cells that the other lacks, or if all cells of one library are shifted in expression, the neighbourhoods are dominated by one library. We summarize this with three measures; the first two are standard metrics of batch mixing in single-cell data~\cite{luecken2022benchmarking}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Rejection rate} (in the style of kBET~\cite{buttner2019kbet}): the fraction of cells whose neighbourhood composition differs from the overall composition (binomial test, $p<0.05$).
	\item \textbf{Local inverse Simpson's index} (LISI~\cite{korsunsky2019harmony}): the effective number of libraries in a neighbourhood (between 1 and 2). It is computed as $1/(p_a^2 + p_b^2)$, where $p_a$ and $p_b$ are the fractions of neighbours from each library, so a 50/50 neighbourhood gives 2 and a single-library neighbourhood gives 1. For simplicity, we weight the 20 neighbours uniformly instead of using the original distance-based weights.
	\item \textbf{Classifier AUC}: a logistic regression predicts the library of a cell from its principal components (5-fold cross-validation). This is a classifier two-sample test~\cite{lopezpaz2017c2st}, a general statistical method rather than an established single-cell metric. A related idea was used by the authors of the atlas, who identified batch-specific gene modules by how well they predict the batch (ROC AUC, via Seurat's \texttt{BatchGene} function)~\cite{farrell2018}. An AUC of 0.5 means the libraries are indistinguishable, 1.0 means they are fully separable.
\end{itemize}
None of these has a natural threshold for ``mixed well'', and with thousands of cells, even a negligible difference becomes statistically significant. We therefore calibrate every pair against reference comparisons of the same size:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Permutation null}: the $a$/$b$ labels are randomly shuffled (200 distinct repetitions) on the same neighbourhood graph. This is what perfect mixing looks like.
	\item \textbf{Negative control}: a random split of one library into two parts of the same sizes as the pair, analysed with the whole pipeline. This is what two samples of the same embryo pool look like.
	\item \textbf{Positive control}: library $a$ against an adjacent stage of the same batch (e.g., 50\% epiboly DS4 against 60\% epiboly DS4). This is what a real developmental difference looks like.
	\item \textbf{Other batch} (where available): library $a$ against the same stage from another batch (50\% epiboly DS3, bud DS3).
\end{itemize}
Finally, we know that the libraries of a pair differ in sequencing depth (up to 3.3$\times$ in the median, Figure~\ref{fig:library-depth}). Normalizing each cell to the same total removes differences in the average expression level, but not in sparsity: a shallow cell has more zeros, which can by itself move it in the embedding. We thus run every comparison twice:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Normalized}: all UMIs, only the per-cell normalization described above.
	\item \textbf{Thinned}: before normalization, each UMI of the deeper library is kept with probability equal to the ratio of the two median depths (binomial thinning~\cite{gerard2020thinning}). Afterwards, both libraries have the same median depth, as if the deeper one had been sequenced less.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Results}

The results are summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:replicate-mixing}. The full per-cell distributions of neighbourhood composition for all comparisons are in Figure~\ref{fig:replicate-mixing-hist}, the UMAPs of all six pairs in Figure~\ref{fig:replicate-mixing-umap} (after thinning: \nolinkurl{results/figures/replicate_mixing_umap_thinned.pdf}), and all numbers in \nolinkurl{results/tables/replicate_mixing.tsv}.
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{The pipeline itself does not separate the libraries.} The random splits are indistinguishable from the permutation null (AUC 0.46--0.54, rejection rate at most 4\%). AUCs slightly below 0.5 are not a systematic effect, but the noise of cross-validation, which goes in either direction.
	\item \textbf{All six pairs are clearly distinguishable.} The classifier AUC is 0.87--1.00, while the highest AUC in the permutation null is 0.52--0.65. Between 12\% and 82\% of cells have a neighbourhood dominated by one library, compared to at most 4\% under the null.
	\item \textbf{The difference is sometimes comparable to a stage step.} At high, $a$ against $b$ is as separable as $a$ against the adjacent stage (AUC 0.97 vs.\ 0.96). At 50\% epiboly, DS4$a$ is as different from DS4$b$ as from DS3 (82\% vs.\ 79\% rejected neighbourhoods). The bud DS2 pair is the most similar (AUC 0.94, 12\% rejected), but there, the libraries of different batches (DS2 against DS3) are also unusually similar (AUC 0.90, 24\% rejected; after thinning even less separable than the pair, AUC 0.77 vs.\ 0.94).
	\item \textbf{Depth is not the cause.} In the normalized experiment, depth alone predicts the library with AUC up to 0.87 (oblong, where library $a$ is 3.3$\times$ deeper; 0.76 at high). After thinning, this drops to 0.48--0.55 in all pairs, but the libraries remain separable (AUC 0.83--1.00).
	\item \textbf{The difference is spread over many genes.} Removing the 1{,}000 genes with the largest $a$/$b$ fold change and repeating the analysis leaves five of the six pairs clearly separable (AUC 0.68--0.78, against at most 0.59 in the permutation null). Only the high pair becomes nearly indistinguishable (AUC 0.60, null up to 0.56).
	\item \textbf{The libraries share their structure, but are shifted within it.} In the UMAPs (Figure~\ref{fig:replicate-mixing}, bottom), both libraries cover the same branches, but within each branch, the cells of $a$ and $b$ occupy neighbouring, mostly non-overlapping regions. This suggests that most of the difference is a shift of the whole library rather than a cell population unique to one of them.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Consequently, merging the $a$/$b$ libraries would mix two distinct expression profiles.} Normalization or thinning does not make them compatible. If we need larger cell pools for some analysis, the two libraries have to be treated like two batches (i.e., with the same care as any other between-library comparison), not as one population. This agrees with the original study, which treated libraries as batches: at 50\% epiboly (four libraries), 7 of the 20 gene modules it identified reflected batch effects and were removed, and the two 6-somite libraries were batch-corrected before trajectory reconstruction~\cite{farrell2018}.

\subsection{Hypotheses: why do the libraries differ?}
\label{sec:replicate-hypotheses}

The analysis above shows \emph{that} the libraries differ, not \emph{why}. Here, we collect the evidence for three plausible explanations. This is not meant to be exhaustive or conclusive. It is based on a pseudobulk comparison of each pair (mean expression per 10{,}000 UMIs, and the fraction of cells in which a gene is detected, computed after thinning the deeper library so that detection rates are comparable). With only one library per side, there is no replicate-level variance, so we do not report $p$-values. Table~\ref{tab:replicate-genes} lists the genes supporting each explanation; all genes with at least a 2-fold difference are in \nolinkurl{results/tables/replicate_de_genes.tsv}.

\paragraph{Different parents (clutch effect).} Each library is a separate pool of embryos, quite possibly from different parents. The supplement of~\cite{farrell2018} only states that the embryos come from wild-type crosses of the Tupfel longfin/AB background, a mix of two laboratory strains, and does not say whether the pools of one stage come from different crosses or days. Wild-type zebrafish lines are genetically heterogeneous, and before and shortly after zygotic genome activation, nearly all mRNA in a cell is inherited from the mother. A genetic difference between parents should show up as \emph{on/off genes}, detected in many cells of one library and almost none of the other, regardless of cell type. Such genes exist in high (e.g., \emph{gbgt1l4}: 6\% vs.\ 71\% of cells) and 50\% epiboly DS4 (e.g., \emph{si:dkey-108k21.10}: 49\% vs.\ 5\%), see Table~\ref{tab:replicate-summary}. At high, library $b$ also contains approx.~2--4$\times$ more of the maternal germ plasm transcripts \emph{nanos3} and \emph{dnd1}, which is consistent with a different maternal contribution. Checking whether the on/off genes lie in genomic regions known for copy-number variation would strengthen this explanation.

\paragraph{Handling stress.} In the 50\% epiboly DS4 pair, library $a$ strongly expresses heat shock genes (\emph{hsp90aa1.2} 16$\times$, \emph{hsp70.3} 9$\times$, \emph{serpinh1b} 7$\times$). \emph{hsp90aa1.2} is detected in 81\% of the cells of library $a$, but only in 14\% of library $b$, and it is high throughout library $a$ in the UMAP (Figure~\ref{fig:replicate-mixing}), i.e., this is a shift of the whole library, not a subpopulation. Heat shock genes respond within minutes to hours, so this most likely reflects the handling of the embryos or cells (temperature, dissociation) rather than development. The protocol leaves room for such differences: dissecting one sample took up to 15 minutes, cells were dissociated mechanically, and droplets were held on ice for up to 1.5 hours to allow several sequential collections before reverse transcription~\cite{farrell2018}, so two libraries of the same stage and batch may have been handled for different lengths of time. Note that this also affects any comparison involving DS4$a$, e.g., heat shock genes appear among the top differences between 50\% and 60\% epiboly in DS4.

\paragraph{Developmental timing.} Embryos of one stage are collected by morphology: each dish was inspected visually, and 20--40 embryos were taken once they reached the desired stage~\cite{farrell2018}. Two pools of the same nominal stage can therefore differ by some minutes of development. We use two independent indicators of developmental time, each expressed in units of one ``stage step'' (the difference between the pair and an adjacent stage of the same batch, so that, e.g., $+0.25$ means that $b$ is a quarter of a stage step older than $a$):
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Ribosomal fraction}: the share of UMIs from the 79 ribosomal protein genes. Across the atlas, it increases monotonically with developmental time, from approx.~2\% at high to 21--24\% at bud, and within a library, it does not correlate with the depth of a cell (median Spearman correlation $-0.05$; \nolinkurl{results/tables/library_ribosomal.tsv}). However, it no longer increases consistently between 3- and 6-somite.
	\item \textbf{Stage-marker score}: the ratio of UMIs in genes that are more than 2$\times$ higher in the older stage to UMIs in genes that are more than 2$\times$ higher in the younger stage (in both libraries of the pair; ribosomal genes excluded). For high and oblong, the reference stage is dome, so this score measures the progress of zygotic genome activation.
\end{itemize}
We consider timing supported only if both indicators agree (Table~\ref{tab:replicate-summary}). This is the case for \textbf{bud DS2, where library $a$ is older} (by 0.5 and 0.2 stage steps). This is consistent with library $a$ containing more cells expressing the muscle genes of the adaxial cells (\emph{myod1}, \emph{myl10}, \emph{actc1a}), which start to express muscle genes during late gastrulation, and fewer cells expressing \emph{sox19b}, an early ectodermal SoxB1 gene. The low detection rates of these genes (at most 21\%) suggest that this is a difference in the number of such cells, as expected if library $a$ is slightly more advanced. Removing the 1{,}000 most different genes does not eliminate the difference (AUC 0.78), so timing explains at most part of it. In 50\% epiboly DS4 (library $b$ older) and oblong (library $a$ older), the indicators agree in direction, but the marker score shift is small. At high and 30\% epiboly, they disagree. In particular, at high, the zygotic score suggests that library $b$ is \emph{younger}, while its ribosomal fraction is higher. At the earliest stages, development changes the ribosomal fraction by less than one percentage point, and in both the high and oblong pairs, the library with the higher ribosomal fraction is the much deeper one, so we suspect a technical component and do not trust this indicator before dome. For 6-somite, neither indicator is usable, because neither changes consistently between 3- and 6-somite.

\paragraph{Unexplained differences.} For 30\% epiboly and 6-somite, we found no specific explanation: both are separable (AUC 0.97 and 0.89), but none of the three explanations is clearly supported. Furthermore, this analysis cannot separate a difference in cell type composition from a shift in expression within cell types. For example, the higher expression of EVL keratins (\emph{krt8}, \emph{krt18}, \emph{cldne}) in oblong $b$ comes with higher detection rates (4--7\% vs.\ 12--21\%), so library $b$ probably simply contains more EVL-like cells. We will revisit this distinction once the cell types are identified.

\section{Method: aligning libraries}
\label{sec:alignment}

Section~\ref{sec:replicates} showed that libraries cannot be pooled: even two libraries of the same stage and batch are separable. Still, we need to compare cells across libraries, e.g., to ask whether a population found in one library is also present in another. We therefore \emph{align} the libraries: we remove what differs between them on average, and keep how their cells differ from each other. This section defines the alignment, tests whether it does what we need, and states when it may be used.

\paragraph{Alignment.} Every library is transformed separately. A UMI count $y$ of a gene in a cell is replaced with its Pearson residual:
\[
	r = \frac{y - s_i m}{\sqrt{s_i m + \varphi_t(m)\,(s_i m)^2}},
\]
i.e., the deviation of the count from what the negative binomial model of the library (Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity}) expects for a homogeneous gene, in units of the expected standard deviation. Here $m$ is the mean of the gene in \emph{this} library, $s_i$ is the sequencing depth of cell $i$ relative to the library average, and $\varphi_t(m)$ is the typical dispersion of a gene of mean $m$ in the library. This is the transformation of sctransform~\cite{hafemeister2019sctransform}, with our own dispersion trend. Residuals are clipped at $\pm 10$, the same limit for every library (a limit that depends on the library size makes two unequal halves of one library look different).

\paragraph{The residuals do most of the alignment.} The model is fitted to each library separately, so the expected count $s_i m$ is built from the library's own gene mean. A gene that is, on average, twice as abundant in library $A$ as in library $B$ has twice the $m$ in $A$, and its residuals measure each cell's deviation from its own library's mean. Since the $s_i$ average to 1, the numerators $y - s_i m$ sum to exactly zero over the library's cells, so every gene has a mean residual close to zero in every library before anything else is done. The difference between library averages, whatever its cause (batch, $a$/$b$, timing, but also the composition of the library), is thereby removed. What remains are deviations from the average, scaled by the noise expected for the gene's mean, so they are comparable between libraries of different depth and dispersion. Fitting one model to the pooled libraries would instead keep the differences between library averages in the residuals.

\paragraph{Centring.} The mean residual is close to zero, but not exactly zero: the denominator gives cells of different depth different weights, and the clip cuts off the largest values of genes expressed in only a few cells. We therefore subtract every gene's mean residual within its library (\emph{centring}). The correction is small: before centring, the mean residual of a heterogeneous gene is a median of 0.02 of its standard deviation (90\% of the genes below 0.11; checked at 50\% epiboly and bud), and only a few sharply restricted genes reach 0.6--0.85. After centring, the average of every gene is exactly zero in every library. Centring shifts all cells of a library by the same amount, so it does not change the distances between them; the residual transformation does change them, as does any normalization.

By \emph{alignment} we mean both steps together: per-library residuals, then centring. We use the genes heterogeneous at the stage; one principal component analysis (30 components) of all libraries of the stage gives shared axes.

\paragraph{What we test.} We compare the aligned data with the unaligned embedding of Section~\ref{sec:replicates} (joint log-normalization and PCA of the compared libraries, on the same genes), in four ways (Figure~\ref{fig:alignment-checks}):
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Libraries of a stage mix.} For every pair of libraries of a stage, the share of cells whose 20 nearest neighbours are significantly unbalanced between the two libraries (as in Section~\ref{sec:replicates}), compared with a random split of one library. Aligned, the random splits give 1--6\% (as unaligned), the $a$/$b$ pairs 1--28\% (median 18\%; unaligned 12--82\%, median 39\%), and pairs from different batches 1--36\% (median 18\%; unaligned 4--95\%, median 63\%). The alignment removes most, but not all, of the difference: in about half of the pairs, 20--36\% of the neighbourhoods remain unbalanced (e.g., 50\% epiboly DS4 $a$/$b$, 6-somite $a$/$b$, 50\% epiboly DS2 vs.\ other batches).
	\item \textbf{Stages stay apart.} Pairs of libraries of different stages from the same batch (so that the batch does not contribute), 800 cells each. Aligned, stages approx.~1\,h apart are blurred (11--44\% unbalanced, median 29\%; unaligned 35--97\%), stages 1.3--2\,h apart mostly separate (57--86\%, median 73\%), and stages more than 2\,h apart separate nearly as well as without alignment (78--96\%, median 92\%). The alignment removes the stage averages too, but cells of distant stages still have no neighbours in common: their populations and gene combinations differ.
	\item \textbf{Structure within a library is kept.} For every cell, the share of its 20 nearest neighbours in the shared PCA that are also among its 20 nearest neighbours in a PCA of its own library alone: 58--100\% (median 79\%). The rest is due to the shared choice of axes. The unaligned joint embedding keeps much less (32--73\%, median 47\%), because its axes also describe the differences between libraries.
	\item \textbf{Cell types find each other.} Six populations with unambiguous markers, at the stages where they are unambiguous (EVL up to shield, primordial germ cells, endoderm, notochord from 75\% epiboly, hatching gland from bud, and the p53-like cells), called in every library from a marker score (positive if above the 99.9\% quantile of the scores of random gene sets with the same expression levels); the marker genes are removed from the features. For every pair of libraries, each cell of one library takes its 20 nearest cells of the other, and we ask how well the share of marker-positive neighbours predicts the cell's own call (AUROC). Aligned, the median AUROC is 0.96 (EVL), 0.99 (endoderm, notochord), 0.98 (hatching gland), 0.88 (p53-like) and 0.71 (PGCs, only approx.~10 cells per library), the same as unaligned. Populations that are distinct in every library are found by both; the alignment does not lose them.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{When to use which.}
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Aligned data} for everything that compares \emph{cells} across libraries: finding a population of one library in another, joint neighbourhoods and pictures of a stage, and links between populations of nearby stages. Even here, two caveats: 20--36\% of the neighbourhoods of some pairs remain unbalanced, so conclusions should not require libraries to be interchangeable, and stages approx.~1\,h apart are partly blurred, so a match between nearby stages is weaker evidence than a match within a stage.
	\item \textbf{Original libraries} for everything about library \emph{averages}: library-level programs, pseudobulk expression, $a$/$b$ differences, timing, and the composition of a library (how many cells a population has). The alignment removes all of these by construction.
\end{itemize}

\section{Result: expression programs}
\label{sec:programs}

An \emph{expression program} is a set of genes associated with one cell type, tissue, cell state or signalling pathway at one stage, e.g., the genes recorded in the notochord at bud, or the feedback targets of BMP. Programs may share genes, and a cell may have several active programs. A program is a hypothesis about co-expression, not a cell type: whether its genes vary together in the cells is tested in Section~\ref{sec:program-tests}. In this section, we define several sets of programs, from the data and from prior knowledge, describe how the curated set is built, when its genes vary between cells, and how strongly each program is expressed in whole libraries. Section~\ref{sec:program-tests} asks whether each program is visible in the data and how much of the variation between cells each set explains, and Section~\ref{sec:signals} relates the programs to the signalling profiles.

\subsection{Program sets}
\label{sec:program-sets}

A program set is a collection of programs that together describe the cells of a stage (Table~\ref{tab:program-sets}; \nolinkurl{scripts/16_program_sets.py}). A program contains only genes measured at the stage (at least 10 UMIs) and has at most 30 genes (the number listed per module by~\cite{farrell2018}); the gene lists of every set are in \nolinkurl{results/tables/program_sets/}. The sets differ in how their programs relate across stages (column ``Programs'' of Table~\ref{tab:program-sets}):
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Per stage, unrelated}: the programs of a stage are unrelated to those of the next.
	\item \textbf{Per stage}: a program keeps its name (an anatomy term, a pathway), but its genes are chosen anew at every stage, and it exists only at the stages where it has enough genes.
	\item \textbf{Fixed}: a program has one gene list, used at every stage, so that its score means the same thing at every stage; only genes not measured at a stage are excluded.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Reference sets.} These are compared with the curated set (and inform future curation decisions) but cannot be used as model phenotypes, because their genes vary per stage:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Farrell et al.\ modules} (per stage, unrelated): the gene modules that~\cite{farrell2018} found by non-negative matrix factorization in each stage of this dataset (their Table~S2: the 30 listed genes of each module, with weights). They were found in these cells, so they are informed solely by data. Consequently, they are are expected to predict the data well, but may not have clear biological interpretations.
	\item \textbf{ZFIN terms} (per stage): one program per anatomy term of ZFIN~\cite{bradford2022zfin}, with the genes that have an in situ (i.e., localized) record in the term at the stage. Broad terms (whole organism, unspecified, \dots) are left out, and a term needs at least 3 genes. Nested terms (e.g., periderm within the EVL, the rhombomeres within the neural plate) are kept as separate programs. \textbf{A second version that keeps only the genes heterogeneous at the stage is also considered}. As a sanity check, the heterogeneous version was also run with the records published before 2018 only, which are independent of the cell atlas; it behaves like the all-years version (Section~\ref{sec:program-support}) and is not reported further.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The curated set} (fixed; used in the rest of the report) combines four kinds of programs, built as described in Section~\ref{sec:program-structure}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Anatomy}: ZFIN terms grouped into curated, named programs, e.g., shield, embryonic shield and organizer into \emph{dorsal organizer}. The result is adjusted to minimize gene overlap between programs. This process is automated, with small but notable human interventions documented in Section~\ref{sec:program-structure}.
	\item \textbf{Signalling profiles}: genes that indicate (a) direct targets of a pathway (transcription directly downstream of the receptor; typically first response); (b) feedback inhibitors (modulate signal strength locally; typically delayed); (c) ligands that carry the signal (a necessary condition for sending it); (d) secreted antagonists and degrading enzymes (which block the signal at short range). Some of these indicate that a cell \emph{receives} the signal (a, b), others that it \emph{sends} (c) or \emph{blocks} (d) it, and some do both (e.g., Nodal ligands are also Nodal targets). Where these genes are present, the pathway has one \emph{general} profile; where not, it has separate \emph{receiving}, \emph{sending} and \emph{blocking} profiles (Section~\ref{sec:program-structure}).
	\item \textbf{Cell states}: maternal transcripts, the pluripotent blastomere state, S and G2/M phases, heat shock and the p53 response (a literature-curated table, \nolinkurl{data/curated/archive/}). These are absent from ZFIN's anatomy terms, but represent behaviors that should be visible in the dataset through heterogeneous genes. They do not necessarily need to form coherent subpopulations (e.g., cell cycle should activate across cell types).
\end{itemize}
The curated programs are disjoint, except along presumed derivations: a program may share genes with the programs it derives from (e.g., the heart with the lateral plate mesoderm, the hindbrain with the neural plate), but not with any other (Section~\ref{sec:program-structure}). The signalling profiles and the cell states are also tested as sets of their own (\textbf{Signalling}, \textbf{Cell states}; fixed, as in the curated set). The literature behind every readout, ligand and antagonist is in Table~\ref{tab:signalling-evidence} (autogenerated from the associated \texttt{.csv} file).

\paragraph{Controls} (fixed) are programs that should not describe cell identity. In other words, it is normal for these to be present, but unusual for them to form well defined subpopulations (or even be heterogeneous in some instances). 
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Whole organism}: genes that ZFIN records in the whole embryo from high to shield and never in a localized structure up to 6-somite (the 30 with the most publications); they should be active, but largely uniform.
	\item \textbf{Silent pathways}: readouts of pathways that should be inactive or uniform at these stages (type I interferon, aryl hydrocarbon receptor, glucocorticoid receptor, circadian clock; Table~\ref{tab:signalling-evidence}).
	\item \textbf{Technical: dissociation stress}: the immediate early genes induced by enzymatic dissociation~\cite{vandenbrink2017}. These are expected to be active in surface cell populations, since they flag cells stressed during sample preparation, but should not form a distinct population. They differ from the heat shock and p53 states: the p53 response takes hours and reflects DNA damage or apoptosis in the embryo before collection (it is a subpopulation across multiple stages and libraries);  meanwhile, heat shock is induced more slowly than the immediate early genes and should be mostly expressed uniformly within a library.
	\item \textbf{YSL}: ZFIN's yolk syncytial layer terms, grouped as the curated programs, without the genes of any curated program or other control. The embryos were deyolked, so YSL cells should be absent, or present only in small numbers.
\end{itemize}
A control for differences in age between the embryos of a library (genes that change between adjacent stages of one batch) was tried and dropped: few genes change between nearby stages, none at all from 60\% epiboly to bud, and the only coherent list was an uncharacterized gene family that marks a population (\emph{si:ch211-113a14}; Section~\ref{sec:program-overlap}), not an age difference.

The number of programs per stage differs between the sources (Figure~\ref{fig:program-set-sizes}): ZFIN terms are few up to 30\% epiboly (1--13 per stage, against 10--17 Farrell et al.\ modules), about as many as the modules from 50\% to 90\% epiboly, and more from bud on (68--110 vs.\ 31--42). Of the 45 curated programs, 39 have a measured gene at high and all from 75\% epiboly on; a heterogeneous gene, 31 at high and 26 at oblong, and 37--45 from dome on.

\subsection{Curated program structure}
\label{sec:program-structure}

The curated set is the one later used to identify subpopulations and candidate phenotypes. Every curated program is defined at every stage, using a shared gene list; The gene list is based on ZFIN and other literature, but the program activity is entirely tied to stages expressing the listed genes (i.e., a program based on ZFIN can be tested also at stages not reported via ZFIN). 

\paragraph{Curation choices.} The curated set is built by \nolinkurl{scripts/16_program_sets.py} from the term mapping \nolinkurl{data/curated/zfin_anatomy_programs.tsv}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Grouping.} ZFIN anatomy terms are grouped into programs that keep their name across stages, e.g., shield, embryonic shield and organizer into \emph{dorsal organizer}. A gene belongs to a group if it has an in situ record in one of the group's terms and, at some stage, the group holds at least a third of the gene's records, so that a gene recorded mostly elsewhere does not join a group through a single record. Genes satisfying this criterion, but recorded in several groups over time are excluded. The groups of terms are defined manually.
	\item \textbf{One gene list per program.} In the grouped ZFIN programs, genes chosen anew at every stage change so much that only the name persists: e.g., an EVL program has 69 different genes over its 10 stages, none of them at all stages. This is not a suitable phenotype definition. Every program therefore has a single gene list, used at every stage: (a) the genes of the group's records from high to 6-somite, pooled; (b) to be included, a gene must be heterogeneous at some stage; (c) genes are ranked by their mean heterogeneity over all stages (0 = not heterogeneous, 1--3 = weak to strong; Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity}), then by their number of ZFIN publications. The list is truncated to 30 genes (median 30; the heart has 5). 
	\item \textbf{Splits.} Groups whose identity changes the most over time are split into smaller programs (column \texttt{fixed\_program} of the mapping table): marginal mesendoderm into \emph{margin} (germ ring, margin) and \emph{mesoderm} (mesoderm, hypoblast), the notochord into \emph{axial mesoderm} and \emph{notochord}, and the prechordal plate into \emph{prechordal plate} and \emph{hatching gland} (polster, hatching gland). This is mostly due to how ZFIN records are organized, where the same term between stages can genuinely represent a different phenotype (i.e., this cannot be addressed simply by more granular grouping).
	\item \textbf{Left out: ZFIN's maternal and blastomere terms.} The maternal records (egg, cleavage) end before the first atlas stage, so there is no ZFIN maternal program. The blastomere terms (blastomere, blastoderm, deep cells, epiblast) collect patterned genes that determine future cell identity, rather than the undifferentiated pluripotent state: even limited to the stages up to shield, the group is led by ventral, non-neural and EVL genes (\emph{gata3}, \emph{tfap2a}, \emph{foxi1}, \emph{vox}, \emph{klf2a}), and later by ventral and marginal genes (\emph{bmp2b}, \emph{gata2a}). The literature sourced programs describe the maternal and blastomere states instead.
	\item \textbf{Signalling evidence.} Every readout, ligand and antagonist has a literature record (Table~\ref{tab:signalling-evidence}; \nolinkurl{data/curated/signalling_evidence.tsv}). Of the 43 readouts, 26 rest on zebrafish evidence of a direct or loss/gain-of-function response (strong) and 17 on indirect zebrafish or other vertebrate evidence (moderate); the 21 ligands and 8 of the 11 antagonists have strong evidence.
	\item \textbf{Signalling profiles.} A profile's score is the mean of its genes (see Section~\ref{sec:program-support}), so a profile is useful only if its genes are expressed in the same cells; a general profile for a pathway whose receivers and blockers are different cells would average genes that are anti-correlated, and ignore both groups. We therefore chose the profiles of each pathway by where the literature places its genes (Table~\ref{tab:signalling-evidence}):
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{General}: \emph{Nodal} (the ligands are themselves Nodal targets, expressed at the margin with \emph{lefty}), \emph{BMP} (the ventral ligands \emph{bmp2b}, \emph{bmp4}, \emph{bmp7a} are BMP targets, expressed where BMP is read), \emph{Wnt} (\emph{wnt8a} and \emph{wnt3a} at the margin, with the readouts) and \emph{FGF} (\emph{fgf8a}, \emph{fgf24}, \emph{fgf3} and \emph{fgf4}, mostly at the margin, with the readouts). Here, sending and receiving a signal cannot be distinguished, so the profile only marks activity. That readouts and ligands mark the same cells is tested by the coherence of the profile: all four are supported at every stage from 60\% epiboly on (Section~\ref{sec:program-support}).
		\item \textbf{Blocking}: the BMP antagonists (\emph{chd}, \emph{nog1/2}, \emph{fsta}), the Wnt antagonists (\emph{dkk1b}, \emph{frzb}, \emph{sfrp1a}) and RA degradation (\emph{cyp26a1}). These cells block a signal for their neighbours and are expected to have low signalling themselves.
		\item \textbf{Sending}: \emph{admp}, the BMP ligand of the organizer (where BMP is low), RA synthesis (\emph{aldh1a2}, in the paraxial mesoderm, away from the cells that degrade RA), the Delta ligands (in lateral inhibition, senders and receivers are neighbouring but different cells), and two pathways without usable readouts: Hedgehog (its readouts are never heterogeneous) and Wnt/PCP (no transcriptional readout).
	\item \textbf{Receiving}: Notch (the \emph{her} and \emph{hey1} targets) and RA (\emph{dhrs3a}, a weak readout).
	\end{itemize}
	Two rules handle genes that do not fit a single profile. \textbf{A gene with two roles in one pathway} goes to the profile where it is expressed (i.e., observable through RNA): \emph{szl} (BMP feedback target and antagonist), \emph{lefty} (Nodal feedback target and antagonist) and the Nodal ligands (also Nodal targets) are expressed where their pathway is active, so they belong to the general BMP and Nodal profiles; \emph{cyp26a1} is induced by RA but expressed where RA is degraded, following anterior identity~\cite{dobbsmcauliffe2004,kudoh2002}, so it marks RA blocking. \textbf{A readout of two pathways} is not used, because its expression cannot be attributed to either: \emph{ved}, \emph{vox} and \emph{vent} (BMP and Wnt8~\cite{ramel2004}), \emph{nkd1} (Wnt and BMP), \emph{nrarpa} (Notch and Wnt) and \emph{nrarpb} (Notch and BMP~\cite{greenfeld2021}). These genes are not reserved for the signalling profiles, so the anatomy programs can still use them (\emph{ved}, \emph{vox} and \emph{vent} are in the margin, mesoderm and tailbud programs). 
	
	As the anatomy programs, a profile is defined at every stage, with the genes of the profile that are heterogeneous at some stage. The expected relations between the separate profiles (e.g., low BMP where BMP is blocked or \emph{admp} is sent, Notch receivers next to Delta senders) are tested in Section~\ref{sec:signal-outputs} (Table~\ref{tab:signal-output-hypotheses}).
	\item \textbf{Disjoint programs.} A gene shared by two programs makes each score respond to the other's cells, so the curated programs share no genes: the genes of the signalling profiles, cell states and pathway controls are not allowed in the anatomy programs, and a gene in two anatomy programs stays only in the one with more ZFIN publications for it (in case of a tie, in the program where it ranks higher). The exception are \textbf{derived programs}: a program may share genes with the programs it derives from, directly or not (column \texttt{derives\_from} of \nolinkurl{data/curated/curated_programs.tsv}; the arrows of Figure~\ref{fig:program-timeline}). The derivations are prior knowledge, an assumption of the model, but none of them is controversial: e.g., the organizer from the margin, the axial mesoderm and the prechordal plate from the organizer, the notochord from the axial mesoderm, the hatching gland from the prechordal plate, the presomitic, lateral plate and intermediate mesoderm from the mesoderm, the heart from the lateral plate, and the neural regions from the neural plate. Without this exception, a program would lose exactly the genes that its precursor passes on. However, this also allows the more differentiated cells to be labeled by their precursor program (i.e., margin program is still active in the dorsal organizer). This underscores the fact that cell identity is rarely a single program, but a \emph{combination} of programs.
	
	Of the genes within the original limit, 91 are excluded from the anatomy programs due to a signalling, state or control program, and 188 were shared with an unrelated anatomy program, 65 of them with equal evidence (Table~\ref{tab:curated-removed}). The programs that shrank most are the primary neurons (30 to 22 genes), the intermediate mesoderm (23 to 15), the organizer, the endoderm (both 30 to 23) and the neural crest (27 to 20).
	\item \textbf{Programs left without specific genes.} Three groups lost most of their markers to the signalling profiles or to other programs, so that what remained was not specific enough. ZFIN has a \emph{ventral mesoderm} term, the ventral side of the dorsal--ventral decision, but its markers are BMP readouts and ligands, so it overlaps BMP signalling too much: what remained were mostly genes with a single publication. The ventral side is described by the general BMP profile instead, and the program is dropped. The \emph{floor plate} lost \emph{shhb} and \emph{lft2} to the Hedgehog and Nodal profiles and its other genes to programs with equal evidence, leaving 2 genes; it is dropped (Hedgehog sending marks the midline). The \emph{presumptive neural} program (presumptive neural plate, neuroectoderm, presumptive brain) mixed anterior and posterior markers (\emph{otx2}, \emph{hoxb1b}, \emph{hoxc10a}) and started after the neural plate; its terms are merged into the neural plate. Two more groups have only three genes, none of them a known marker, and are left out: the head mesoderm (\emph{tpm4a}, \emph{cdc42ep4a}, \emph{prrx1a}) and the hemato-vascular group (\emph{has2}, \emph{ets2}, \emph{ef1}; recorded from 3-somite).
	\item \textbf{Manual assignments.} A gene can be assigned to a program against its ZFIN records (\nolinkurl{data/curated/gene_overrides.tsv}, with reason and references). This is used once: \emph{dharma} (\emph{bozozok}), the earliest organizer gene~\cite{yamanaka1998}, which ZFIN records mostly in the YSL (it is expressed in the dorsal YSL beneath the shield). It is assigned to the dorsal organizer and so leaves the YSL control; it is heterogeneous from high to dome, before the organizer's ZFIN window.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What each program is.} Table~\ref{tab:curated-programs} (supplementary) describes every curated program: its source, the program of Table~\ref{tab:programs} it corresponds to (the curated set extends Table~\ref{tab:programs} beyond the early decisions, e.g., to the neural regions, somites and neural crest), the programs it is presumed to derive from, where and under which signals it arises, why it is part of the model and how to interpret it. The annotation is kept in \nolinkurl{data/curated/curated_programs.tsv}, and the table is generated from it (\nolinkurl{scripts/24_program_overview.py}).

\subsection{Curated programs over time}
\label{sec:program-pseudobulk}

\paragraph{Expression overview.} Figure~\ref{fig:program-timeline} shows the curated programs over the stages: how many of each program's genes are heterogeneous at each stage and how strongly (their mean tier, per Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity}), the stages at which ZFIN records the program (its \emph{ZFIN window}: from the first to the last stage with at least 3 of the group's genes recorded), and, as indentation and arrows, the presumed derivations between programs. It compares the literature (the windows) with the data (the marker heterogeneity). ZFIN records no anatomy program at high and oblong, only the EVL and the margin at dome, 7 programs at 30\% epiboly, 11 from 50\% to 60\% epiboly, 15--16 at 75--90\% epiboly and 22--23 from bud on. In the data, the programs vary much earlier: 12 of the 25 anatomy programs have at least 3 heterogeneous genes at high (8 at oblong, with fewer cells), and 21--25 from dome on. Inside its ZFIN window, a median of 81\% of a program's genes are heterogeneous (62\% at the worst stage, 50\% epiboly); outside it, 43\%. In other words, gene heterogeneity is associated with ZFIN documented activity windows, but can appear earlier and last beyond the reported window. The reasons are discussed with the observations below.

\paragraph{Observations.} Several features of the two figures are worth noting:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Programs vary before ZFIN records them.} Most anatomy programs have heterogeneous genes before their ZFIN window. Some of this is expected: the PGCs are specified by maternal germ plasm (\emph{dnd1}, \emph{nanos3}, \emph{ddx4}) from the start, and the organizer genes (\emph{gsc}, \emph{dharma}) are maternal $\beta$-catenin targets on the dorsal side; a structure is usually recorded only once it is morphologically visible, after its genes start to vary. Some of it is shared genes: a program can only be scored on genes it shares with its precursors, e.g., the hatching gland and the prechordal plate. And some of it may be genes that vary for unrelated reasons; whether the genes of a program vary \emph{together} is tested later in Section~\ref{sec:program-support}. Over whole libraries, many anatomy programs keep a strength of 0.2--0.4 before their ZFIN window, because some of their genes are also expressed elsewhere at lower levels; they are stronger inside the window than outside it (median 0.45 against 0.27), and most rise towards the end of the time course. The strength compares a program with itself over time, not different programs.
	\item \textbf{Programs vary after ZFIN stops recording them.} The organizer (ZFIN: up to 75\% epiboly) and the margin (up to bud) keep many heterogeneous genes up to 6-somite. Programs are not expected to switch off when a new one appears: the organizer and the margin give rise to the axial mesoderm and the tailbud, which keep using some of their genes, which explains why we see them continuous active.
	\item \textbf{Maternal and blastomere states} have almost no heterogeneous genes at the early stages: they are expressed in nearly every cell, so they do not vary between cells. The blastomere state has heterogeneous genes from 60\% epiboly on, through \emph{sox3}, \emph{sox19a} and \emph{pou5f3}, which are then expressed in the neural plate. Over whole libraries (Figure~\ref{fig:program-pseudobulk}), the maternal state falls from 0.96 (high) to 0.25 (dome) and below 0.1 from 50\% epiboly: the clearance of maternal transcripts. The blastomere state stays at 0.5--0.65 up to shield and falls to 0.22 by 6-somite. Within these early libraries, maternal and blastomere programs are the most expressed ones, supporting the assumption that they are uniformly active.
	\item \textbf{Leaders.} The strength compares a program only with itself; which program dominates a stage is shown by the leaders (ringed in Figure~\ref{fig:program-pseudobulk}): in each library, the program of each kind whose genes have the highest mean expression; a program leads a stage if it leads in at least one of its libraries. The mean over genes, not their sum, is used, so that long gene lists are not favoured. Leaders are counted per library rather than per stage, because the mean expression of a program's genes differs by a median of 22\% between the libraries of a stage. The maternal and blastomere states are compared with the anatomy programs, so that the shift between them shows: the maternal state leads at high and oblong, the blastomere state from dome to shield (in every library), the blastomere state and the neural plate share 60\% epiboly (one and two libraries), and the neural plate leads from 75\% epiboly on, with the tailbud in one of the two 6-somite libraries. Among the other cell states, the S phase leads at every stage; among the signalling profiles, BMP, with RA blocking at high and in some libraries at 90\% epiboly and bud. A leader has abundant genes, which is not the same as many cells: small programs, such as the heart or the PGCs, never lead, but may matter as much.
	\item \textbf{PGCs and the p53 response.} The PGC program (0.91 at high, below 0.1 from 60\% epiboly) contains maternal germ plasm transcripts. It is high early on and falls as other cell types take over. The p53 response is strongest at high and oblong (0.70--0.74), where it is also strongly heterogeneous (\emph{cdkn1a}, \emph{phlda3}, \emph{gadd45aa}).
	\item \textbf{Early dorsal genes.} Some signalling profiles are heterogeneous from high on through a single strongly heterogeneous gene: \emph{chd}, \emph{dkk1b}, \emph{ndr1}, \emph{lft1} and \emph{wnt11}. These are targets of maternal $\beta$-catenin on the dorsal side and among the first zygotic genes.
	\item \textbf{Signalling profiles} rise and fall in the expected order: Nodal peaks at 30\% epiboly (0.83) and is about 0.1 from 3-somite; RA synthesis peaks at 60\% epiboly and RA receiving at bud; Notch receiving and Hedgehog sending rise towards the segmentation stages.
	\item \textbf{Cell cycle.} The S and G2/M states become heterogeneous only from 75\% epiboly (G2/M) and bud (S) on, consistent with the cell cycle lengthening and becoming asynchronous during gastrulation.
	\item \textbf{The YSL control} has many strongly heterogeneous genes from 50\% epiboly on, although the embryos were deyolked. Whether these genes mark YSL-like cells is tested in Section~\ref{sec:program-support}.
	\item \textbf{Single-library stages} (shield, 3-somite) have fewer heterogeneous genes in several programs (e.g., the axial mesoderm at shield, the PGCs at 3-somite): with one library and fewer cells, the heterogeneity test has less power and by extension fewer strong genes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Conclusions}

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{The curated set} has 45 programs (25 anatomy programs from grouped ZFIN terms, 14 signalling profiles and 6 cell states), each with one gene list that is used at every stage. The programs share no genes, except along presumed derivations (prior knowledge, e.g., the hindbrain from the neural plate).
	\item \textbf{Literature and data differ in timing.} The genes of most anatomy programs vary between cells stages before ZFIN records the program (12 of 25 have at least 3 heterogeneous genes at high), and some keep varying after ZFIN stops recording them. Inside its ZFIN window, a median of 81\% of a program's genes are heterogeneous; outside it, 43\%.
	\item \textbf{Whole libraries} are dominated first by maternal transcripts (high, oblong), then by the blastomere state (dome to shield), and then by the neural plate, joined by the tailbud at the somite stages. Programs that are expressed evenly in all cells, such as the maternal and early blastomere states, are visible only at this level.
\end{itemize}

\section{Result: expression programs describe the cells}
\label{sec:program-tests}

Here we test the programs against the cells: whether the genes of each program vary together within the libraries (Section~\ref{sec:program-support}), how much of the variation between cells each set explains (Section~\ref{sec:program-prediction}), and how many activity levels a program needs to keep that information (Section~\ref{sec:program-levels}).

\subsection{Is a program visible in the data?}
\label{sec:program-support}

\paragraph{Scores.} The score of a program in a cell is the mean of the Pearson residuals of its genes under the model of the cell's library (Section~\ref{sec:alignment}), centred per library (weighted for the Farrell et al.\ modules). Centring (subtracting each gene's mean residual in the library, so that every gene has mean zero in every library) is required to make residuals comparable across libraries. The residuals already account for sequencing depth, so, unlike the usual gene-set score~\cite{wolf2018scanpy}, no control genes are needed, and the score has the same meaning in aligned and per-library data. Since the residuals are centred per library, the score describes differences between the cells of a library; a program that is active at the same level in every cell of a library is constant (zero) within it, even if its level differs between libraries.

If the genes of a program vary together in the cells, the program is visible in the data. We measure this by the coherence
\[
	R = \frac{n \operatorname{Var}(\text{score})}{\text{mean variance of a gene's residuals}} \approx 1 + (n-1)\,\bar\rho,
\]
where $n$ is the number of genes and $\bar\rho$ their mean correlation. $\operatorname{Var}(\text{score})$ is the variance of the program's score across the cells of a library (the score being the mean of the residuals of the program's genes). The approximation is exact if all genes have the same variance. $R = 1$ if the genes vary independently: averaging $n$ independent genes divides the variance by $n$, so $n \operatorname{Var}(\text{score})$ equals the mean gene variance. If the genes rise and fall together, averaging does not cancel their variation, and $R$ grows up to $n$.

\paragraph{Supported programs}

To determine which programs vary together significantly, we could use an obvious null: shuffling every gene independently across the cells. However, this is too weak: random sets of heterogeneous genes pass it in 74\% (50\% epiboly) and 27\% (bud) of cases, because heterogeneous genes vary along a few shared axes of the stage (e.g., margin vs.\ animal pole). In other words, the shuffle removes all co-variation, but almost any set of heterogeneous genes has some, so passing it does not show that the genes of a program belong together. We therefore require a program to be more coherent than 95\% of 500 random sets of heterogeneous genes of the same size, in the same library, and call it \textbf{supported} at a stage if this holds in at least half of the stage's libraries (\nolinkurl{scripts/17_program_tests.py}).

\paragraph{Relation to standard tests.} $R$ is (approximately) a rescaled mean pairwise correlation of the program's genes ($\bar\rho = (R-1)/(n-1)$), so it orders programs as the measures usually used for a co-expression module do, e.g., the mean correlation or Cronbach's $\alpha = n\bar\rho/R$~\cite{cronbach1951alpha}; the variance form needs one variance per program instead of all pairwise correlations. The two nulls are the two kinds of gene-set test~\cite{goeman2007genesets}: the shuffle is a \emph{self-contained} test (do the genes co-vary at all?), the random sets a \emph{competitive} one (do they co-vary more than other genes?), as in gene set enrichment analysis~\cite{subramanian2005gsea}. Our competitive null draws only heterogeneous genes, so a program has to beat genes that vary anyway. Unlike co-expression measured on log-normalised counts, the residuals remove sequencing depth first, which otherwise makes all genes correlate positively~\cite{hafemeister2019sctransform}, and the test runs within each library, so differences between libraries do not appear as co-expression.

\paragraph{Relative coherence score}

The support decision is a test; to see how coherence changes over time, also below the threshold, we use the \textbf{relative coherence} $(R - \tilde R_\text{random}) / (R_\text{random}^{95\%} - \tilde R_\text{random})$, with $\tilde R_\text{random}$ the median and $R_\text{random}^{95\%}$ the 95\% quantile of the random sets of the same size in the same library, and its median over the stage's libraries (Figure~\ref{fig:program-coherence}). It is 0 for a program as coherent as a typical random set of heterogeneous genes, 1 at the threshold of support, and negative for a program less coherent than random heterogeneous genes (negative values are common in stages where the program's genes are not heterogeneous). It allows us to assess coherence relatively across stages and between programs.

Every curated program is tested at every stage, also outside its ZFIN window. From 60\% epiboly on, 56--73\% of the curated programs are supported (Farrell et al.: 53--81\%, ZFIN terms: 54--68\%, restricted to heterogeneous genes: 62--75\%); up to shield, only 7--19\% (Farrell et al.\ modules: 18--36\% at dome to shield). Here, the inability to detect coherent programs in early stages largely lies in the strictness of the test: before gastrulation, the heterogeneous genes vary along the same few axes (few genes at all up to dome), so the 500 random sets are nearly as coherent as the actual programs. In fact, the typical anatomy program is less coherent than random heterogeneous genes up to 50\% epiboly (median relative coherence $-0.26$ to $-0.50$ per stage) and about as coherent at shield (0.01); its genes vary, but not more together than any genes that follow the main axes of the stage. From 60\% epiboly on, the median rises to 1.1--3.6 (Figure~\ref{fig:program-coherence}). The signalling profiles are the exception: up to shield, they are weakly above random (median 0.30), mostly below the threshold. Removing the unsupported programs at these stages therefore loses part of the predictive power (at dome and 50\% epiboly, more than later; Section~\ref{sec:program-prediction}): they describe real variation that the strict supported program test cannot conclusively distinguish from that of random heterogeneous genes.

\paragraph{Literature and data.} For the anatomy programs, support follows the ZFIN window, but not exactly. Inside the window, 74\% of a program's stages are supported (73--91\% from 60\% epiboly on), outside it 16\% (6\% up to shield, 44\% from 60\% epiboly on). Programs that are coherent outside their window are those anticipated in Section~\ref{sec:program-pseudobulk}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Before ZFIN records them}: the EVL and the PGCs at high and oblong (the PGCs also at dome; maternal germ plasm), the non-neural ectoderm at oblong and 60\% epiboly, and the precursors of programs that ZFIN records only from bud: the notochord, the hatching gland and the adaxial somites from 60\% to 90\% epiboly, the heart at bud and 3-somite. The relative coherence shows these as trends: the notochord rises from $-0.3$ at shield to 1.1 at 60\% epiboly, 3.7 at 90\% epiboly and 8.3 at bud, and the heart from about 0 at 90\% epiboly to 1.2 at bud.
	\item \textbf{After ZFIN stops recording them}: the organizer from 90\% epiboly to 6-somite and the margin at 3- and 6-somite, consistent with programs that persist in the tissues they give rise to (the axial mesoderm, the tailbud).
\end{itemize}

Inside the window, the unsupported programs are mostly broad or early but with rising coherence (e.g., early endoderm and mesoderm). Notably, neural plate is never supported. We hypothesize that this is because the program is very general (covers a wide range of precursors) and broadly expressed, so it suffers from similar problems as the maternal and blastomere programs early on (by Figure~\ref{fig:program-pseudobulk}, it is the most expressed program from 60\% epiboly onward). The other curated programs unsupported from 60\% epiboly on are expected: maternal transcripts (gone), heat shock (uniform), the p53 response from bud on, the RA profiles and most sending and blocking profiles (see Section~\ref{sec:signal-outputs}).

\paragraph{Controls.} The control programs that should be invisible are: the readouts of the interferon, aryl hydrocarbon receptor, glucocorticoid and circadian pathways, and the whole-organism genes are not coherent in any library at any stage. Two controls are clearly visible, and both matter for future analysis (the YSL is discussed below):
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Dissociation stress.} The immediate early genes (\emph{fosab}, \emph{junba}, \emph{atf3}, \emph{egr1}) are coherent in every library from shield on (and in one of the two high libraries): part of the cells responded to the dissociation, as in other tissues~\cite{vandenbrink2017}. This is a technical axis of variation, and also one of the unnamed Farrell et al.\ modules at shield (Figure~\ref{fig:program-overlap}); it should be discarded as technical outlier.
	\item \textbf{The YSL.} The embryos were deyolked, which removes the YSL, so its genes should not form a visible program; the YSL is therefore a control. ZFIN's YSL term is coherent in no library up to 3-somite and in one of the two at 6-somite; restricted to heterogeneous genes, it is supported at 60\% epiboly, bud and the somite stages, through secretory and ER genes such as \emph{hspa5} and \emph{sec61a1l}, which are expressed in many tissues. The YSL control leaves out every gene of the curated programs and other controls (12--29 genes per stage, led by \emph{apoeb}, \emph{tnfaip8l2b}, \emph{tfa} and \emph{sesn3}); it is coherent in no library up to 90\% epiboly, in two of four at bud and in one library at 3- and at 6-somite. So a small YSL-like signal appears at the somite stages, as expected if a few YSL nuclei or yolk-attached cells remain after deyolking, but not before.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{How much of the variation does each set explain?}
\label{sec:program-prediction}

\paragraph{The question.} If a program set describes the states of the cells, then knowing a cell's program scores should tell us about the expression of genes that are in none of the programs. We test this at every stage by predicting held-out genes from the program scores, and compare each set with a model that knows only sequencing depth and library (the \emph{floor}) and with the best model we could find (the \emph{ceiling}). The test runs on all cells of the stage; its steps (\nolinkurl{scripts/17_program_tests.py}):
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Evaluation genes} are genes detected in 2--98\% of the stage's cells that belong to no program of any set at the stage (Farrell et al., ZFIN terms, signalling, cell states, curated programs, controls), so that no set predicts its own genes. They are taken in a fixed random order, in two groups: up to 400 heterogeneous genes (116--233 per stage from dome on, except shield: 49, since most heterogeneous genes are in some program) and 400 other measured genes (\emph{all}).
	\item \textbf{Target.} For each cell and evaluation gene: the share of the cell and its 10 nearest neighbours in its library that detect the gene (at least one UMI). A single cell detects a gene or not largely by chance; the neighbourhood frequency is a less noisy estimate of its expression. The neighbours are found among the evaluation genes themselves, so the target does not share neighbourhoods with the features.
	\item \textbf{Features.} The score of every program of the set (Section~\ref{sec:program-support}), averaged over the cell and its 10 nearest neighbours in its library (neighbours in the principal components of the stage's heterogeneous genes that are not evaluation genes).
	\item \textbf{Models.} For each evaluation gene, a logistic regression on 70\% of the cells (training, stratified by library), with L2 regularization chosen by 3-fold cross-validation, so that uninformative features are shrunk towards the floor. The models differ only in their inputs: \emph{floor} (smoothed log depth and library); each \emph{program set}, i.e., the floor plus the set's scores (also the supported curated programs alone, and the random sets of step 6); and three models without programs: the 30 smoothed \emph{principal components} of the heterogeneous genes that are not evaluation genes, \emph{all} (principal components, curated and Farrell et al.\ scores together), and \emph{nearest neighbours} (the gene's detection frequency among the cell's 20 nearest training cells).
	\item \textbf{Score.} The log-loss on the other 30\% of the cells (test), summed over the evaluation genes of a group. The \emph{ceiling} is the model with the lowest loss at the stage, whichever it is. The \textbf{explained fraction} of a set is (loss$_\text{floor}$ $-$ loss$_\text{set}$)/(loss$_\text{floor}$ $-$ loss$_\text{ceiling}$): 0 means no better than depth and library, 1 as good as the best model. The \textbf{explainable share}, (loss$_\text{floor}$ $-$ loss$_\text{ceiling}$)/loss$_\text{floor}$, says how much of the variation there is to explain at all. The same losses are also summed over the test cells of each library (with the stage's ceiling model; libraries with fewer than 100 test cells get no explained fraction), which shows whether the libraries of a stage agree.
	\item \textbf{Random reference.} For every program, a set of as many random genes with the same expression levels (50 bins of mean expression; never evaluation genes) is scored in the same way; a set should explain more than its random counterpart.
\end{enumerate}
\paragraph{How much can be explained.} Little of the variation of other measured genes can be explained by anything: the best model removes at most 6\% of the floor loss (Figure~\ref{fig:program-tests}a), and we report explained fractions only where at least 4\% can be explained. Heterogeneous genes are much more predictable: 24--36\% of the floor loss from dome on, except shield (6\%); at high and oblong almost nothing (1\% or less). The predictability of a gene follows its heterogeneity (Figure~\ref{fig:program-tests}d): pooled over the stages from dome on, 4\% of the floor loss of evaluation genes that are not heterogeneous can be explained, 22\% of weak, 34\% of moderate and 49\% of strong heterogeneous genes. Most measured genes vary between cells only by chance, so there is little to predict about them; the heterogeneous ones carry the cell states. Shield is the exception: its heterogeneous evaluation genes are barely predictable in every tier (strong ones: 6\%, against 27--70\% at the other stages from dome on). The likely reason is the test itself: the neighbourhoods of the target are defined by the evaluation genes, and with 49 heterogeneous ones they carry little structure; the single library is not the reason (3-somite, also a single library, with fewer UMIs per cell: 24\%). The ceiling is usually one of the feature sets (the Farrell et al.\ modules up to 30\% epiboly and at 3-somite, the supported curated programs at shield, the principal components or all features together otherwise), never the nearest-neighbour model.

\paragraph{Results} Figure~\ref{fig:program-tests}b, heterogeneous genes, dome to 6-somite:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Every set explains far more than random genes.} Farrell et al.\ explains 0.92--1.00, ZFIN terms 0.86--0.99, curated 0.84--0.98, vs.\ 0.33--0.90 for random sets matched to the curated programs. Relative to random ((set $-$ random)/(1 $-$ random)): curated 0.60--0.91, ZFIN terms 0.54--0.97, Farrell et al.\ 0.73--1.00. The same holds for the other measured genes where defined (curated 0.83--0.97 vs.\ random 0.27--0.64, from 60\% epiboly on).
	\item \textbf{Restricting to heterogeneous genes costs nothing.} ZFIN terms restricted to heterogeneous genes explain as much (0.92--0.99) with a third fewer genes.
	\item \textbf{The curated set is as good as the ZFIN terms with fewer programs.} With 41--45 programs per stage from dome on (ZFIN terms: 8 at dome, up to 110 at 6-somite), it explains 0.96--0.97 up to 50\% epiboly and 0.94--0.98 from 60\% epiboly on (ZFIN terms: 0.94--0.99 and 0.91--0.97); at shield, both explain less (0.84 and 0.86). The Farrell et al.\ modules remain the best, as expected from their in-sample advantage.
	\item \textbf{Unsupported programs matter little.} From 60\% epiboly on, the supported curated programs alone (25--33 per stage) explain 0.93--0.97 (all curated: 0.94--0.98). Up to 50\% epiboly, with only 3--4 supported programs, they lose more at dome and 50\% epiboly (0.94 vs.\ 0.96; 0.91 vs.\ 0.96), but not at 30\% epiboly (0.99 vs.\ 0.97).
	\item \textbf{Signalling and states alone} explain 0.41--0.94 and 0.32--0.88: they are a few programs each, which describe a part of the variation that overlaps the identity programs.
	\item \textbf{The controls explain much early, little late}: 0.61--0.83 from dome to 50\% epiboly, at most 0.42 from 60\% epiboly on. Before gastrulation, even genes that should not mark identity (whole-organism genes, silent pathways) follow the few shared axes of variation, which is another sign that these stages lack distinct programs.
	\item \textbf{The libraries of a stage agree} (bars in Figure~\ref{fig:program-tests}a, b): the explainable share of the heterogeneous genes differs by at most 0.09 between the libraries of a stage, and the curated programs explain 0.94--0.98 in every library from dome on except shield (0.84), within 0.02 of each other at each stage; for every set, the libraries of a stage differ by at most 0.06 (random sets: 0.09), so the bars of Figure~\ref{fig:program-tests}b are mostly hidden by the markers. The explainable share does not follow the size or depth of a library (Spearman correlation over the 24 libraries from dome on: $-0.14$ with the number of cells, 0.20 with the median UMIs per cell): the smallest bud library with a test (DS2$b$, 506 cells) reaches 0.37, the largest (DS3, 4{,}759 cells, with the fewest UMIs per cell) 0.33. For other measured genes, the explainable share follows the number of heterogeneous genes of the library ($\rho = 0.66$): little of a random gene is predictable while the cells are alike, and more as distinct cell states appear.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{How many activity levels does a program need?}
\label{sec:program-levels}

A Boolean or multi-valued model describes every program in a cell by one of a few levels. We ask how much of what the continuous scores say about a cell survives when every curated program is reduced to $k$ levels (\nolinkurl{scripts/22_program_levels.py}). We reuse the prediction test of Section~\ref{sec:program-prediction} with the same cells, split, smoothing and target, but with evaluation genes of its own. Only the curated programs are tested here, so an evaluation gene needs to be in no curated program, but may be in a program of the other sets; this gives more and stronger heterogeneous evaluation genes (187--388 per stage from dome on, shield: 111, against 49 in Section~\ref{sec:program-prediction}). The floor and the ceiling (the best of the nearest-neighbour model, the principal components, the continuous scores and their levels) are fitted on these genes, so the explained fractions differ from those of Figure~\ref{fig:program-tests}; what we compare is each discretisation with the continuous scores. The features:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{$k$ levels} ($k$ = 2--6): the smoothed scores of each program are cut into $k$ levels by 1-D $k$-means of the training cells, pooled over the stage's (aligned) libraries, i.e., one set of thresholds per program and stage, as a model would use; every cell gets the level of its nearest centre.
	\item \textbf{Boolean (null)}: a program is \emph{active} in a cell if its smoothed score is higher than chance allows. The chance level comes from a null in which the program's genes do not vary together: in each library, every gene's residuals are shuffled independently across the cells (each gene keeps its own values, but which cells are high in one gene no longer relates to the others), the shuffled residuals are scored and smoothed exactly like the real ones, and this is repeated 5 times. The threshold is the 99.9\% quantile of these null scores, so by chance only 1 in 1{,}000 cells would be called active; it is computed per program and library, so a program of a few sparse genes needs a stronger signal than one of many well-expressed genes. \textbf{3 states}: in addition, \emph{depleted} below the 0.1\% quantile of the same null. This is the call of ``active'' used elsewhere in the report (e.g., Section~\ref{sec:program-populations}); unlike the coherence test (Section~\ref{sec:program-support}), it does not ask whether a program is real, only whether it is clearly on in a given cell.
\end{itemize}
A program with $k$ levels enters the regression as $k-1$ indicators ``level $\geq v$''. This allows any effect of every level, but the regularization of the regression shrinks a level towards the one below it rather than towards the lowest; indicators ``level $= v$'' span the same models but are worse with many levels and few cells (e.g., 6 levels at shield: 0.56 vs.\ 0.75 of the continuous result). The levels of a program also allow effects that are not linear in its score, so a discretised set can sometimes explain slightly \emph{more} than the continuous scores.

\paragraph{Result} Figure~\ref{fig:program-levels}; neighbourhood target, heterogeneous evaluation genes, dome to 6-somite; ``kept'' = explained fraction relative to the continuous scores, which explain 0.95--0.99:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Two levels keep over 85\%} (median 0.94, range 0.86--0.97). \textbf{Three levels keep nearly everything} (median 0.97, 0.87--0.99), and four or five are as good (median 0.98); from 60\% epiboly on, 3 levels keep 0.97--0.99 and 4 levels 0.98--1.01.
	\item \textbf{Where the threshold lies matters as much as the number of levels.} The Boolean call from the permutation null keeps only 0.90 (0.79--0.94), less than two $k$-means levels: its strict threshold marks only cells that are clearly active, and cells with an intermediate score fall into ``off''. Adding the depleted state recovers part of it (3 states: 0.94, 0.72--0.96), but less than three $k$-means levels.
	\item \textbf{Shield loses most.} At shield (one library, 1{,}046 cells), 2 levels keep 0.86, 3 levels 0.87, and more levels lose (4 levels: 0.84, 5 levels: 0.77), although the continuous scores explain as much as at the other stages (0.96). The test is weaker there: with more evaluation genes than in Section~\ref{sec:program-prediction}, 15\% of the floor loss can be explained (6\% there), but still much less than at the other stages from dome on (29--43\%); with little to explain, extra indicators mostly fit the noise of the training cells. The single library is not the reason: the other single-library stages lose little (3 levels: dome 0.94, 3-somite 0.98).
	\item \textbf{The supported programs alone, with 3 levels}, keep 0.94--0.98 of the continuous curated set from 60\% epiboly on, so restricting a model to them costs little; the broad unsupported programs (mesoderm, neural plate; Section~\ref{sec:program-support}) still carry information.
	\item \textbf{Random measured genes} show the same order with larger losses: from 50\% epiboly on (where defined), 2 levels keep 0.80--0.92, 3 levels 0.82--0.96 and the Boolean call 0.70--0.83.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Which programs are switches?} Separately from the prediction, we measure how much of the variance of a program's smoothed score its $k$-means levels keep (Figure~\ref{fig:program-levels}c). Two levels keep a median of 69\% of the variance (3 levels: 85\%, 4 levels: 91\%), much less than they keep of the prediction: what matters for the other genes is mostly which cells are at the top, and the variation within the bulk of the cells matters less. Per program (median over all stages), the programs that come closest to a switch (2 levels keep 74--87\% of the variance, and the upper level holds 1--10\% of the cells) are distinct populations and localized signalling centres: the EVL, hatching gland, endoderm and non-neural ectoderm, the Hedgehog senders, RA synthesis, the BMP antagonists, \emph{admp} and Nodal. The most graded are the cell cycle (2 levels split the cells about in half), the uniform states (maternal, p53 response, heat shock), and the broad anatomy programs (dorsal forerunner, neural plate and its regions, lateral plate; 55--65\%), as expected for gradients and continuous processes.

\paragraph{Caveats.} The levels are computed from scores smoothed over each cell's 10 nearest neighbours, so they describe a cell's neighbourhood rather than the cell itself; levels of unsmoothed scores would be noisier, and we have not tested them. The thresholds are fitted per stage, so a program's levels need not mean the same at two stages, and they are fitted on aligned scores, so they say nothing about differences between whole libraries (e.g., the maternal program). Finally, the test measures what the levels say about other genes, not whether they are the right variables of a dynamical model.

\paragraph{Conclusion for the model.} Three levels per program (low / intermediate / high), with thresholds that are placed by the data rather than by a strict significance cut-off, keep practically all of what the continuous scores explain; a Boolean description keeps over 90\% with data-placed thresholds and slightly less with the significance call. The exception is shield, at the focus of the model, where 3 levels keep only 0.87; most likely because the test has little to explain there (15\% of the floor loss), not because of its single library. The two-level description is close to complete for programs of distinct populations, and loses most for graded programs (signalling, cell cycle), which are the natural candidates for more than two levels.

\subsection{Conclusions}

Programs from prior knowledge describe the cells nearly as well as the modules found in the data, and far better than random gene sets. Restricting them to heterogeneous genes loses nothing, and a first curated set of 39--45 named programs, each with one gene list for all stages, is as useful as the ZFIN terms. From 60\% epiboly on, more than half of the curated programs are clearly visible in the data, three quarters of the anatomy programs within their ZFIN window; the rest are expected to be invisible (maternal, uniform states), too broad (neural plate), or not yet or no longer formed. Several programs are visible before ZFIN records them (precursors of the notochord, hatching gland, adaxial somites and heart) or after (organizer, margin). Before 60\% epiboly, the variation between cells is dominated by a few shared axes, and our test cannot tell individual programs apart. The libraries of a stage agree: the curated programs explain 0.94--0.98 in every library from dome on except shield, independently of its size and depth. How much of a gene's variation can be explained at all follows its heterogeneity (4\% for genes that are not heterogeneous, 49\% for strongly heterogeneous ones), and grows with the number of distinct cell states, not with sequencing; shield, with few heterogeneous evaluation genes, is barely testable. For a discrete model, three data-placed levels per program keep nearly all of the information of the continuous scores, and two levels about 90\% (Section~\ref{sec:program-levels}).

\section{Result: programs and signals}
\label{sec:signals}

The signalling profiles of the curated set (Section~\ref{sec:program-structure}) mark where a pathway is active, or where a signal is sent or blocked. Here we relate them to the identity and state programs: which tissues take part in which signal, and when (Section~\ref{sec:signal-coupling}); whether the expected signalling centres are where the literature puts them (Section~\ref{sec:signal-outputs}); and which signals accompany the emergence of a program from the program it derives from (Section~\ref{sec:signal-transitions}). All three analyses follow the rules of Section~\ref{sec:program-tests}: they are computed within each library, and every number is compared with random sets of heterogeneous genes (\nolinkurl{scripts/21_signal_coupling.py}).

\subsection{Which programs co-occur with which signals?}
\label{sec:signal-coupling}

The general and receiving profiles measure where a pathway is active, so their correlation with the identity and state programs tells us which populations take part in which signal, and when. Within each library, we correlate the smoothed scores of each profile with those of every program over the cells. The curated programs are disjoint (Section~\ref{sec:program-structure}), so no correlation comes from shared genes. A raw correlation is, however, hard to read: the smoothing raises all correlations, and before gastrulation almost any two sets of heterogeneous genes correlate, because they vary along the same few axes (Section~\ref{sec:program-support}). We therefore compare each correlation with those of 100 random sets of background genes (heterogeneous at the stage, in no curated program) of the profile's size, scored and smoothed in the same way. As for the coherence, the \textbf{relative coupling} is 0 for a profile as coupled to the program as random genes, and $+1$ or $-1$ at the 95\% or 5\% quantile of the random sets; per stage we report its median over the libraries, and whether every library is beyond the random sets on the same side (Figure~\ref{fig:signal-coupling}). For a general profile (Nodal, BMP, Wnt, FGF), a coupled program sends or receives the signal, or both; a coupling says that the pathway's genes are expressed in the program's cells, not that the signal causes the program.

The baseline removes the couplings that any heterogeneous genes would show: the Notch readouts, for example, correlate at $r = 0.65$--$0.96$ with the EVL, non-neural ectoderm, neural crest and hatching gland programs from dome to 50\% epiboly, but their relative coupling is at most 1.0. It does not remove the main axis itself: before shield, the profiles that mark the margin (Nodal, FGF, Wnt, and BMP at the ventral margin) are coupled, positively or negatively, with nearly every program that lies on one side of the animal--vegetal axis (Figure~\ref{fig:signal-coupling}), so the early couplings mostly say on which side of that axis a program's genes are expressed. Most couplings agree with what is known about these pathways in the zebrafish gastrula:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Nodal} is coupled to the margin, the endoderm and the mesoderm at dome (relative coupling 2.4--3.1), fading after 50\% epiboly: Nodal signals are required for endoderm and most mesoderm~\cite{feldman1998,gritsman1999}, and the duration of signalling during the blastula and early gastrula decides the fate~\cite{hagos2007}. It stays longer in the dorsal organizer (1.5--2.6 up to 75\% epiboly), the prechordal plate (2.4 at shield) and the axial mesoderm (1.2--1.5 from dome to 60\% epiboly), as expected from the highest Nodal requirement of the prechordal plate~\cite{gritsman2000} and Nodal ligands and \emph{lefty} in axial tissues~\cite{rebagliati1998,thisse1999}. The blastomere program is anti-coupled ($-1.2$ to $-2.6$ from dome to shield): cells that receive Nodal have lost the pluripotency genes.
	\item \textbf{BMP} is coupled to the non-neural ectoderm and the placodes (1.2--1.8 from 60\% epiboly to bud) and the neural crest (1.0--1.4 from 75\% epiboly on), and anti-coupled to the neural plate, the forebrain and the midbrain--hindbrain boundary (up to $-6$ at 30\% epiboly, $-1.1$ to $-2.4$ from shield to 75\% epiboly, weaker later): the BMP gradient of dorsoventral patterning~\cite{hammerschmidt1996,kishimoto1997,nguyen1998}. Before shield, the BMP profile is coupled to the tailbud program (1.9--3.7 from dome to 50\% epiboly), i.e., to the ventral margin, where the BMP ligands are expressed, and against the whole ectoderm, including the non-neural ectoderm; the tail requires BMP~\cite{pyati2005}.
	\item \textbf{Wnt} is coupled to the margin and the mesoderm (1.9--2.8 from dome to 50\% epiboly), the presomitic mesoderm and the tailbud (up to 3.0; the tailbud from dome to 75\% epiboly and at the somite stages), and anti-coupled to the forebrain and eye ($-1.2$ to $-1.7$ from 30\% epiboly to bud): Wnt8 at the ventrolateral margin is required for ventrolateral mesoderm and posterior fates~\cite{lekven2001,thorpe2005}, and the forebrain requires Wnt to be off~\cite{kim2000,heisenberg2001}.
	\item \textbf{FGF} is coupled to the margin, the organizer, the presomitic mesoderm and the axial mesoderm (1.8--2.9 from dome to 50\% epiboly), and to the tailbud at every stage from dome on except bud, and anti-coupled to the blastomere program ($-1.9$ to $-2.7$ from dome to 50\% epiboly): FGF at the margin is required for trunk and tail mesoderm~\cite{griffin1995,draper2003}, where its feedback genes are expressed~\cite{furthauer2001,raible2001}.
	\item \textbf{Notch} (receiving) is coupled to the tailbud, increasingly from 60\% epiboly (0.5) to 6-somite (1.7).
	\item \textbf{RA} (receiving, a single gene, \emph{dhrs3a}) is coupled to the hindbrain (1.1--1.8 at 75\% and 90\% epiboly), as expected from RA in the hindbrain, and to the blastomere program, through its neural genes. A single gene has no coherence to test (Section~\ref{sec:program-support}), but its coupling can be tested like any other.
\end{itemize}
Some expected couplings are missing, and some are unexplained:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Notch is not coupled to the presomitic mesoderm (at most 0.4 from shield on), although the segmentation clock depends on Notch~\cite{holley2002}; the clock genes oscillate, so at any moment only part of the presomitic cells express the readouts. Notch is also not coupled to the primary neurons (at most 0.6), although \emph{her4} is a Notch target in the proneural clusters~\cite{takke1999}. FGF is anti-coupled to the midbrain--hindbrain boundary ($-0.9$ to $-1.0$ from 60\% to 90\% epiboly), although Fgf8 is made there~\cite{reifers1998}: the general FGF profile is dominated by the margin and the tailbud (Section~\ref{sec:signal-outputs}).
	\item Hedgehog readouts (\emph{ptch1/2}, \emph{gli1}, \emph{hhip}) are never heterogeneous, so Hedgehog has only a sending profile, and its reception by the floor plate and adaxial cells cannot be tested.
	\item The maternal program is coupled to Nodal and FGF from 30\% epiboly to shield (1.0--2.9 in every library), so maternal transcripts are higher where the margin profiles are; whether the marginal cells keep maternal transcripts longer is open. The coupling of Nodal with heat shock (1.1--1.2 at 60\% and 75\% epiboly) differs between libraries ($-0.6$ to 1.4 at 75\% epiboly) and is likely technical.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Which tissues send and block which signals?}
\label{sec:signal-outputs}

The general profiles above mark where a pathway is active; the sending and blocking profiles mark where a signal is sent or blocked by cells that need not receive it (Section~\ref{sec:program-structure}). We test the expected senders, receivers and blockers explicitly (Table~\ref{tab:signal-output-hypotheses}). A correlation over all cells misses small source populations, such as the organizer among all gastrula cells, so each hypothesis is tested on the cells in which the tissue's program is active (Section~\ref{sec:program-levels}). The \textbf{AUROC} says whether the profile's score is higher in these cells than in the others: 0.5 means no difference, 1 that every active cell scores higher than every other cell, 0 the reverse. The other cells are, where the program has parent programs (Figure~\ref{fig:program-timeline}), the active cells of the parents: whether the organizer sends \emph{admp} is asked against the rest of the margin, not against the whole embryo, so that a profile shared by the whole margin does not count for the organizer. As for the couplings, every AUROC is compared with random sets of the profile's size (relative AUROC, median over the libraries), and a hypothesis is tested only at the stages at which the tissue's program is supported. A hypothesis is \emph{consistent} if it is beyond the random genes on the expected side at one stage or more and never on the other side.

Of the 24 hypotheses, 19 are consistent, 3 weak (never beyond the random genes) and 2 opposite:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{The expected tissues send, receive and block the expected signals}, also against their parent programs: Nodal, Wnt and FGF at the margin (best AUROC 0.97--0.99), BMP in the non-neural ectoderm (0.82--1.00 from 60\% epiboly on), Wnt and FGF in the tailbud (0.88, 0.73), the RA-synthesizing \emph{aldh1a2} in the presomitic mesoderm (0.83) and the RA-degrading \emph{cyp26a1} in the tailbud and the forebrain (0.88, 0.79), Delta ligands in the presomitic mesoderm and primary neurons (0.88, 0.77), the organizer's \emph{admp} (0.83), the BMP and Wnt antagonists in the organizer (0.95, 0.83) and the Wnt antagonists in the prechordal plate (0.90).
	\item \textbf{Delta senders are next to Notch receivers.} Cells with active Delta have high Notch readouts (AUROC up to 0.84, from bud on), although the two profiles are separate: in lateral inhibition, the cells that send and receive are neighbours, which the smoothing over neighbourhoods brings together.
	\item \textbf{Cells that block BMP have low BMP signalling} (AUROC 0.16 at dome and 30\% epiboly, 0.22--0.36 from 75\% epiboly to 3-somite), and so do the cells that make the organizer's BMP ligand \emph{admp} (best 0.15, median 0.17): the organizer sends a BMP ligand but is itself shielded from BMP, consistent with ADMP acting at a distance~\cite{lele2001,willot2002}.
	\item \textbf{RA is made and degraded in different cells} at 90\% epiboly and bud (AUROC down to 0.22).
	\item \textbf{Weak against the parent}: Nodal in the endoderm (median AUROC 0.62 against the margin, which also receives Nodal), Hedgehog in the notochord (0.68 against the axial mesoderm, which also makes \emph{shha}) and FGF at the midbrain--hindbrain boundary (0.33 against the neural plate: the boundary has \emph{lower} FGF scores than the rest of the neural plate).
	\item \textbf{Opposite}: Hedgehog in the axial mesoderm against the organizer (bud), since \emph{shha} is already made in the organizer, and the Wnt antagonists against Wnt, which share the margin at 30\% epiboly (AUROC 0.99 there) before they separate (down to 0.10 at 3-somite, not beyond the random genes).
\end{itemize}
The sending and blocking profiles are rarely coherent (Section~\ref{sec:program-support}; supported: Hedgehog from 60\% epiboly, Delta from bud, the BMP antagonists from shield to 75\% epiboly, the Wnt antagonists and Wnt/PCP at 60\% epiboly): most pool genes of several tissues, e.g., \emph{chd} of the organizer and \emph{fsta} of the paraxial mesoderm, or have one or two genes. They are best read as markers of signalling centres within a tissue, together with the identity programs. Their couplings with every program are in Figure~\ref{fig:signal-outputs}.

\subsection{Which signals accompany a transition?}
\label{sec:signal-transitions}

The derivations between programs (Figure~\ref{fig:program-timeline}) are presumed transitions between phenotypes, e.g., blastomere $\rightarrow$ margin. A Boolean model needs, for each transition, the signals under which it happens. For every derivation, we compare every signalling profile between the cells in which the derived program is active and the other active cells of its parent (relative AUROC, as above), at the first three stages at which the derived program is supported, i.e., at its emergence. A profile that is higher in the derived cells at two of these stages or more, and never lower, is a candidate input of the transition; a lower one a candidate inhibitor. Table~\ref{tab:transitions} lists them next to the signals expected from the annotation of the programs (Table~\ref{tab:curated-programs}). Of the 28 transitions, 26 can be tested (the neural plate and the dorsal forerunner cells are never supported):
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Confirmed}: blastomere $\rightarrow$ margin with higher Nodal and FGF; margin $\rightarrow$ dorsal organizer with BMP and Wnt antagonists, \emph{admp} and Hedgehog ligands, and lower Wnt; margin $\rightarrow$ tailbud with higher Wnt, FGF and BMP (and RA degradation); blastomere $\rightarrow$ non-neural ectoderm with higher BMP and lower BMP antagonists, Wnt and FGF; neural plate $\rightarrow$ forebrain with lower Wnt and higher RA degradation; neural plate $\rightarrow$ neural crest with higher BMP and Wnt (non-neural ectoderm $\rightarrow$ neural crest: Wnt); dorsal organizer $\rightarrow$ prechordal plate with lower Wnt and FGF.
	\item \textbf{Partly confirmed}: mesoderm $\rightarrow$ presomitic mesoderm with higher Nodal and RA synthesis and lower BMP, but not the expected Wnt, FGF and Notch; neural plate $\rightarrow$ placodes with higher BMP, but lower FGF, which the preplacodal ectoderm needs~\cite{kwon2010}; neural plate $\rightarrow$ hindbrain with lower RA degradation only.
	\item \textbf{Not seen}: BMP for the lateral plate, FGF for the midbrain--hindbrain boundary (which has higher Wnt), Notch for the primary neurons, Nodal for the endoderm and the prechordal plate against their parents (which also receive Nodal), and Hedgehog for the adaxial cells (no readouts).
	\item \textbf{No signal expected, none found}: maternal $\rightarrow$ PGC (germ plasm) and blastomere $\rightarrow$ EVL (position).
\end{itemize}
These are candidate rules, not proven causes: a signal can differ between two populations because one of them makes it, or because the two lie in different places. Where expected inputs are missing, the profile may lack readouts in the relevant tissue (Hedgehog, the Notch oscillator), or the parent receives the same signal (Nodal).

\subsection{Conclusions}

Compared with random genes, most couplings between the signalling profiles and the identity programs agree with the zebrafish literature: Nodal at the margin and in the endoderm, then along the axis; BMP first at the ventral margin, then in ventral and non-neural fates and against the neural plate; Wnt and FGF at the margin and in the posterior mesoderm. Of 24 expected signalling centres, 19 are consistent when tested against the neighbouring (parent) tissue at the stages at which the tissue is visible. For the transitions between programs, the data confirm the expected signals of the earliest decisions (margin, organizer, tailbud, non-neural ectoderm, forebrain, neural crest) and miss some later ones (Notch, FGF at the midbrain--hindbrain boundary, BMP in the lateral plate). Correlations show co-expression, not causation, and the sending and blocking profiles are best read as markers of signalling centres within a tissue; the RA and Hedgehog pathways have few or no usable readouts.

\section{Result: cell populations}
\label{sec:populations}

Clustering a single library always yields clusters, whether or not the cells form distinct groups, and such clusters are often poorly separated and do not recur across libraries. Here, a cluster counts as a \emph{population} only if it is found independently in another library. We find populations twice: from the heterogeneous genes, and from the program scores of Section~\ref{sec:program-support}; comparing the two tells us which populations the programs describe and which programs are missing.

\subsection{Replicated populations}

\paragraph{Procedure} (\nolinkurl{scripts/14_replicated_populations.py}). Every library is clustered on its own (Leiden~\cite{traag2019leiden} on a 15-nearest-neighbour graph, resolutions 0.5, 1, 2 and 4), in the aligned space of its stage (Section~\ref{sec:alignment}). Clusters of two libraries are compared by neighbour voting, as in MetaNeighbor~\cite{crow2018metaneighbor}: every cell of library $B$ takes its 10 nearest cells of library $A$, and the vote of an $A$ cluster is the share of them in it. The AUROC of the vote for a $B$ cluster (its cells vs.\ the rest of $B$) says how well the $A$ cluster picks it out. Two clusters \emph{match} if each is the other's best counterpart and each still wins (AUROC $\geq$ 0.8) when compared with the runner-up only, which separates a true counterpart from a merely related cluster. Populations are the groups of matching clusters; populations of different resolutions that share at least half of their cells are merged, and a population is \textbf{robust} if found at two or more resolutions. Stages with a single library (dome, shield, 3-somite) are matched against the libraries of the adjacent stages, which is weaker evidence (Section~\ref{sec:alignment}).

\paragraph{Controls.} Arbitrary clusters of the same number, built around random cells (so compact, but meaningless), and matched in the same way, give 19--31\% as many populations as the real clusters (per resolution, summed over stages; most at resolution 4). With another random seed, 95--97\% of the robust populations are found again (Jaccard $\geq$ 0.5), but only 56\% of the populations found at a single resolution.

\paragraph{Result.} We find 131 robust populations: none at high and oblong, 3--6 from dome to 50\% epiboly, 11--17 from shield to 90\% epiboly, 25 at bud, 23 at 3-somite and 17 at 6-somite. Their top genes name known populations without any marker list: e.g., the EVL (dome to shield, and 3- and 6-somite), the p53-like cells, the dorsal organizer, which becomes the prechordal plate (\emph{gsc}, \emph{frzb}) and then the hatching gland (\emph{he1a}, \emph{ctslb}), the notochord (\emph{noto}, \emph{twist2}, \emph{ntd5}), the endoderm (\emph{sox32}, \emph{sox17}), the tailbud and presomitic mesoderm, primordial germ cells (75\% epiboly), and, by 3-somite, the eye field, the midbrain--hindbrain boundary, hindbrain rhombomeres, the neural crest, placodes, somites, adaxial and early muscle cells, primary neurons and blood/endothelial progenitors. From shield on, 47--94\% of the cells (median library) belong to a robust population; from dome to 50\% epiboly, only 13--28\% (none at high and oblong). Populations of consecutive stages are linked when each picks out the other in a joint analysis of the two stages (AUROC $\geq$ 0.9), e.g., the endoderm from 50\% epiboly to 6-somite (Figure~\ref{fig:population-tracks}); since the alignment blurs stages approx.~1\,h apart, a link means ``most similar population of the next stage'' rather than proof of descent.

\subsection{Populations from programs}
\label{sec:program-populations}

\paragraph{Procedure} (\nolinkurl{scripts/18_program_populations.py}). The same procedure, but every cell is described by the scores of the curated programs that are supported at its stage, smoothed over its 10 nearest neighbours in its library and scaled to unit variance. A program-based population is described by its top programs. A gene-based robust population is \emph{captured} if a robust program-based population shares at least 30\% of its cells (Jaccard). If not, it is \emph{named, not separated} if at least two of its top 8 genes belong to one curated program of the stage (the program exists but does not separate the population from its neighbours), and \emph{unnamed} otherwise: a candidate missing program.

\paragraph{Result.} The program scores give 1--24 robust populations per stage; the arbitrary clusters give 31--44\% as many, more than for the gene-based populations, so program-based populations are less specific. Of the 131 gene-based populations, 71 are captured, 53 are named but not separated, and 7 are unnamed (Figure~\ref{fig:program-populations}b). With the programs restricted to their ZFIN windows, the numbers were 70, 51 and 10; with stage-specific gene lists, 81, 40 and 10: programs with one gene list for many stages (mesoderm, neural plate) are less often supported, and so separate fewer populations.
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Named, not separated}: mostly parts of broad curated programs, e.g., the ventrolateral and posterior mesoderm, presomitic mesoderm and tailbud within \emph{margin} and \emph{mesoderm}, the forebrain and midbrain--hindbrain boundary within \emph{neural plate}, non-neural and placodal populations named by both the \emph{non-neural ectoderm} and the \emph{placodes} (which share genes such as \emph{foxi1}), and subpopulations of the EVL. The curated programs are too coarse to separate these populations; the ZFIN terms (or our population genes) are finer.
	\item \textbf{Unnamed}: the dissociation-stress cells at shield (\emph{fosab}, \emph{atf3}, \emph{junba}; a control, not a curated program); a small p53-like population at shield (\emph{sesn3}, \emph{bcl2l14}, \emph{gadd45aa}); a family of uncharacterized genes (\emph{si:ch211-113a14.*}, \emph{zgc:110425}, \dots) at dome and shield, which may be technical; a posterior non-neural population at bud (\emph{apoc1}, \emph{apoeb}, \emph{msx1b}, \emph{cdx4}, \emph{tfap2a}, \emph{dlx3b}) and a related one at 6-somite (\emph{apoeb}, \emph{apoc1}, \emph{crabp2a}, \emph{cdx4}; ZFIN: periderm); and a hindbrain neuron population at 6-somite (\emph{hoxd4a}, \emph{hoxa3a}, \emph{sox3}, \emph{dla}, \emph{myt1a}) (Table~\ref{tab:missing-programs}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Coverage.} From 60\% epiboly on, 65--97\% of the cells (median library) have at least one active program (smoothed score above the 99.9\% quantile of a gene-wise permutation within the library), and 47--94\% belong to a robust gene-based population (Figure~\ref{fig:program-populations}a). Up to 50\% epiboly, only 10--25\% of the cells have an active cell-level program, and 0--28\% belong to a robust population: at these stages, most cells differ from each other along graded axes rather than by belonging to distinct populations, and the dominant programs (maternal, blastomere) act on whole libraries.

\subsection{Conclusions}

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Alignment.} Pearson residuals under each library's own model, centred per library, make cells of different libraries comparable without making the libraries interchangeable; it is to be used for comparing cells, not library averages.
	\item \textbf{Programs.} All program sets explain far more of the variation between cells than random genes; restricting them to heterogeneous genes costs nothing; and a first curated set of 39--45 named programs, each with one gene list for all stages, is as good as the ZFIN terms. From 60\% epiboly on, more than half of the curated programs are clearly visible in the data, and several are visible before or after ZFIN records them.
	\item \textbf{Populations.} Requiring a cluster to recur in another library gives 131 robust, reproducible populations, which name themselves through their genes. The curated programs capture 71 of them as program-based populations. Most of the rest lie inside programs that are too broad (margin, mesoderm, neural plate), and seven have no program.
	\item \textbf{Before 60\% epiboly}, cells mostly differ along shared graded axes; programs and populations describe only a minority of the cells, and the main programs act on whole libraries.
	\item \textbf{Next steps for the curated set}: split the remaining broad programs (mesoderm into paraxial, lateral and intermediate parts before bud; neural plate into regions), using ZFIN terms or the gene-based populations; separate the non-neural ectoderm from the placodes, which share genes through their derivation; add programs for the remaining unnamed populations; and use about three activity levels per program (Section~\ref{sec:program-levels}).
\end{itemize}
